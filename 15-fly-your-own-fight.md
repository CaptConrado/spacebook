**CHAPTER THIRTEEN**

# Fly Your Own Fight

I WANT to tell you the most important thing I know about combat flying.

You cannot fly like somebody else.

You are not somebody else. You have your own reflexes, your own ship, your own strengths, and your own weaknesses. You have a certain number of hours in the cockpit, a certain amount of combat experience, and a certain temperament. These factors, taken together, define the kind of pilot you are and the kind of fight you can win.

The fight you can win is not the same fight that somebody else can win. And if you try to fly the fight that somebody else can win, instead of the fight that you can win, you will lose.

This is the lesson that I most frequently see young pilots fail to learn. They have been trained by instructors who were aces. They have studied the flying of the great pilots of previous generations. They have watched combat footage of spectacular engagements. And they step into the cockpit believing that their job is to fly the way those pilots flew.

It is not.

Your job is to fly the way you fly, as well as you can fly, within the limitations of who you are and what you are flying.


## The Comparison Trap

I call this the comparison trap, and it is the single most common error I have observed in young pilots.

A rookie joins their squadron. They look around at the veterans. They see one pilot who specializes in aggressive knife-fight engagements, closing to minimum range and using short bursts to devastating effect. They see another pilot who is a finesse flyer, using the vertical and energy management to outmaneuver opponents who cannot keep up with their geometry. They see a third pilot who is a long-range specialist, using their ship’s endurance to outlast enemies and pick them off when they run low on fuel.

The rookie wants to be all three of these pilots at once.

They cannot be. Nobody can be. Each of these veterans has developed a specific style over years of flying, and each style is rooted in the veteran’s specific strengths. The aggressive pilot is probably a natural gunner with fast reflexes. The finesse pilot is probably someone who came up in a ship that rewarded energy management. The long-range pilot is probably someone whose temperament favors patience over aggression.

The rookie has no way of knowing which of these styles, if any, matches their own strengths. But if they try to emulate all three, they will be bad at all three. They will be a less effective version of the aggressive pilot, and a less effective version of the finesse pilot, and a less effective version of the long-range pilot. They will not have developed their own style, the style that fits their actual strengths, because they have been too busy imitating others.

This is how promising pilots become mediocre.


## Finding Your Style

How do you find your own style?

The short answer is: you fly a lot, and you pay attention to what works for you.

The longer answer requires some self-examination.

Ask yourself what parts of flying come most naturally to you. Are you a good gunner, who can track a target across complex geometry and land hits that other pilots miss? Are you a good energy manager, who has an instinctive feel for the state of your fuel and altitude? Are you a good tactician, who can see the larger engagement and position yourself advantageously? Are you a good communicator, who keeps their wingman informed and responds quickly to incoming calls?

Every pilot has natural strengths. Yours are there, if you will look for them.

Now ask yourself what parts of flying are hard for you. Not humiliatingly hard, every pilot has struggled with everything at some point, but chronically hard. The things that, no matter how much you practice, never quite become automatic. Your weaknesses are there too, and they matter just as much as your strengths.

Your style of flying, once you have identified your strengths and weaknesses, is the style that maximizes the former and works around the latter.

If you are a good gunner but a mediocre energy manager, your style should probably be aggressive, closing quickly, taking the shot, breaking off before your energy deficit becomes critical. You cannot outlast opponents in prolonged engagements, so you do not try. You end fights quickly.

If you are a good energy manager but a mediocre gunner, your style should probably be patient, working the geometry, waiting for the easy shot, never risking a deflection shot you might miss. You cannot win shooting contests, so you do not try. You win positional contests instead.

If you are a good tactician, you should lead. You should be the flight lead when your squadron permits it, using your tactical sense to position the entire flight advantageously.

If you are a good communicator, you make an outstanding wingman, because wingmen who call threats clearly and maintain formation discipline save lives.

Every pilot has a style that works for them. Your job, in your first few years of flying, is to find yours.


## The Ace Is Not You

Let me speak directly to a specific kind of young pilot for a moment.

If you joined the service because of a specific ace you admired, a pilot whose exploits you read about, or whose combat footage you studied, or whose memoir you wore out from reading, I want to say something to you.

You are not that pilot.

I mean this with no disrespect to you or to your hero. I mean it as a matter of simple fact. The ace you admire was a specific person, with specific strengths, who flew a specific ship in a specific campaign against specific enemies. The conditions that produced their success will not be repeated for you. You will fly different ships, in different campaigns, against different enemies, under different operational constraints.

If you try to replicate their style, you will fail, because the circumstances that made their style successful are not your circumstances.

What you can do is take from their example the things that are universal, the discipline, the professionalism, the commitment to the craft, and apply them to the development of your own style.

The great aces of history did not become great aces by imitating earlier aces. They became great aces by flying their own fight, in their own way, against their own enemies. They are models of professional development, not templates to be copied.

Honor your heroes by becoming your own kind of pilot. That is what they would want. That is what every ace I ever knew told the rookies who asked them how to be great.

Fly your own fight. It is the only one you have.


## When Your Style Changes

One more point, and then I will let this chapter end.

Your style will change over the course of your career.

This is normal. It is expected. It is, in many ways, a sign of your development as a pilot.

The pilot you are in your second year of combat flying is not the pilot you will be in your tenth year. Your reflexes change. Your judgment deepens. Your body ages, and with age comes the loss of some capabilities and the acquisition of others. The styles that worked for you when you were young may not work for you when you are older, and you must be willing to adjust.

I flew aggressive knife-fight engagements in my twenties. By the time I was forty, my reflexes were not quite what they had been, and I could no longer count on winning the quick-draw contests I had previously dominated. I had to develop a new style, more patient, more positional, relying on my deepening tactical sense rather than on raw speed.

If I had refused to adjust, if I had insisted on flying the way I had flown in my twenties, I would have been killed in my forties. Many pilots are killed that way. They fly the style that used to work, long after it has stopped working, and they do not live to become old.

Be willing to change. Be willing to admit that the pilot you were is not the pilot you are. Develop new styles as your circumstances change.

The goal is not to fly like the pilot you were or the pilot you admire. The goal is to fly like the pilot you are, today, with the ship you are flying today, against the enemies you are facing today.

That is the fight you can win.

That is the only fight worth trying to win.
