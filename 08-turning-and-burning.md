**CHAPTER SIX**

# Turning and Burning

THE turn, properly understood, is a transaction.

You are trading energy for angle.

A great many rookies, and a depressing number of journeymen, do not understand this. They think of the turn as a maneuver, a thing you do with the stick, and they evaluate it by how tight it is and how fast it is and how impressive it looks to the pilots watching. They do not think about what the turn is costing them.

Every turn costs you energy.

Every turn you make reduces your capacity to make subsequent turns, and eventually, if you turn enough without replenishing your energy state, you will have nothing left to turn with. You will be in a slow, flat, sad little spiral through the engagement zone, and the enemy, who has managed their energy better, will come up behind you and shoot you down.

This is the most common way in which energy-ignorant pilots die.

They turn themselves to death.


## The Sustained Turn vs. the Instantaneous Turn

There are two kinds of turn, and you must understand the difference between them, because mixing them up will kill you.

The instantaneous turn is the tightest turn your ship can make at a given moment. It is a turn that uses the maximum available lift and the maximum available thrust, and it gives you the fastest possible change of heading. An instantaneous turn is devastatingly useful for things like defensive breaks, evading a missile, or snapping your guns onto a fleeting target.

The cost of the instantaneous turn is enormous. You bleed energy fast. You cannot sustain the turn for more than a few seconds before your ship is dragging, your speed has dropped, and your options have collapsed. An instantaneous turn is a one-shot weapon. You use it when you absolutely must, and you pay the price, and you recover.

The sustained turn is a turn at your ship’s best sustained turn rate, the rate at which the ship can maintain its velocity and energy state indefinitely. A sustained turn is slower, broader, and less dramatic than an instantaneous turn. It does not look as impressive on guncam footage. It will not make the gray-haired pilots in the debriefing room nod in appreciation.

But the sustained turn is the turn that wins dogfights.

A pilot who knows their ship’s sustained turn rate, and who disciplines themselves to fly at that rate in extended engagements, will have more energy than their opponent throughout the fight. They will be turning just as fast as the enemy who is burning at the instantaneous rate, after the first thirty seconds, because the enemy will have bled off and slowed down. And they will still have energy in the bank when the enemy has nothing left.

The rule I teach is this. You use the instantaneous turn for the crisis, and you use the sustained turn for the fight.

The crisis is a moment, a moment in which you must turn right now or die. A missile in the final seconds of its terminal phase. An enemy whose guns are about to track onto your ship. The crisis justifies the cost of the instantaneous turn because the alternative is worse.

The fight is everything else. The long minutes of maneuvering, the circling, the positional contest that makes up ninety-five percent of any engagement. In the fight, you turn at the sustained rate. Always. Always. Always.

Rookies turn at the instantaneous rate the entire fight, because it feels right, because it feels like they are doing something, because their reflexes are telling them to pull harder and faster. They feel like heroes in the cockpit, and they are dead in two minutes.

Discipline yourself.


## The Vertical

I will tell you something now that I wish someone had told me when I was young.

The vertical is your friend.

Most rookies fight as if they are in an atmosphere, as if their only options for maneuver are in the flat plane. They turn left, they turn right, they circle. They treat altitude as a number on their instrument panel, not as a dimension of the fight.

This is a catastrophic error.

A fighter ship does not live in two dimensions. It lives in three, and the third dimension, the vertical, is where you make your money. A pilot who uses the vertical well has access to a whole library of maneuvers that a flat-plane pilot does not have. They can dive for energy. They can climb to commit. They can reverse through the vertical. They can put their opponent beneath them, or above them, or passing through their sight picture at angles their opponent did not think to defend.

When you are outnumbered, use the vertical.

When you are outgunned, use the vertical.

When you are outmatched in pure performance, use the vertical.

The pilot who thinks in three dimensions will almost always beat the pilot who thinks in two, even if the two-dimensional pilot is flying a faster ship or has better guns. The third dimension is a free gift to the pilot who remembers they have it.

I have killed pilots who, on paper, should have killed me. I killed them because they thought of the sky as a plane. I thought of it as a sphere. And while they were circling me, trying to get a shot in the horizontal, I was climbing above them, or diving below them, or inverting through the vertical to take a shot they never saw coming.

The vertical does not look dramatic on a flat tactical display. It does not show up well in after-action reports. Young pilots who watch combat footage often do not even notice it is happening.

But the vertical is where the aces live.

Learn it. Use it. Think in three dimensions or die in two.


## Altitude Is Energy, in a Well

Near a planet or a moon, altitude is energy.

I mean this in the most literal, physical sense. A ship deep in a gravity well, but high within it, carries potential energy that a dive will convert into velocity for free, paid for by the world's pull rather than by your propellant. A ship lower in the same well has already spent that potential, and must buy it back by climbing, and climbing out of a well is paid for in reaction mass, which is to say in the one currency you cannot make more of once you have launched.

This means that in any engagement fought inside a well, the pilot who enters higher holds an energy advantage. They can dive to accelerate without spending a gram of propellant. They can trade height for position. They can treat their altitude as a reserve and draw on it exactly when they choose.

A pilot who enters lower is fighting at a structural disadvantage. They can overcome it, good pilots do, but they have to work harder, burn more, and take more risk to do it.

But understand the limit of this, because it is the kind of thing that kills a pilot who learned the rule without learning its edges. Altitude is energy only where there is a well to fall into. In deep space, far from any world, there is no altitude. There is no up, no down, no free velocity waiting to be spent. There is only your vector and the propellant to change it, and a pilot who flies the open dark as though height still meant something is a pilot reaching for a reserve that is not there. The well is a gift when you are in one. Out in the deep, you have only what is in your tanks, and you had better know which kind of sky you are fighting in before the fight starts, not during it.

Inside a well, this has direct tactical implications.

If you are approaching an engagement inside a well, climb before you commit. Get above the enemy and stay there. Do not arrive at the merge at the same height if you can help it. Arrive higher. The height is worth more than the propellant you spent buying it, because you can spend it back into velocity the instant you need it, and the well does the spending for you.

Conversely, when the enemy holds the height and you do not, do not fight them where they are. Climb first to match them, or drag the fight lower in the well where their advantage thins, or break it off and force the next one onto open ground where height means nothing to either of you. What you must never do is grind out a fight from below while they work from above. In a well, that is a fight you will lose. The only mercy of the deep dark is that out there, this particular way of dying does not exist, because out there nobody holds the height. Know which sky you are in.


## The Reversal

One more concept before I let you go on to the gunnery chapter, because this is a concept that rookies almost always get wrong.

The reversal is the moment in a dogfight when both pilots change direction simultaneously, typically to bring guns to bear on each other. It is the natural outcome of a sustained turning fight, two pilots circling, each trying to get behind the other, eventually reaching a geometry where a reversal is the logical next move.

The temptation, for a rookie, is to reverse as soon as they see the opportunity, because the reversal feels aggressive. They think they are taking the initiative.

What they are actually doing is committing to a pattern their opponent has already planned for.

An experienced pilot will set up a reversal deliberately. They will create the conditions that make a reversal look attractive to their opponent, knowing that when the opponent reverses, the geometry will briefly favor the experienced pilot, and in that brief moment, the shot will be taken.

The defense against this is discipline. You do not reverse just because an opportunity to reverse has appeared. You reverse only when the geometry genuinely favors you, when your speed is higher, your energy state is better, and your angular position is superior. If any of those are not true, you do not reverse. You continue to work the turn, you rebuild your energy, and you wait for a moment when the reversal is actually to your advantage.

A reversal that is not to your advantage is a gift to your opponent.

Do not give gifts to your opponent.
