_PART FOUR: THE LONG WAR_

**CHAPTER FOURTEEN**

# The Hangover of Victory

THERE is a moment, after a pilot scores a significant victory, when they are in more danger than they have been in for some time.

Not during the victory. During the aftermath.

I have watched this pattern over and over, across campaigns and across generations of pilots. A pilot has a great mission. They get two or three kills. They fly beautifully. They return to the hangar to the backslaps and handshakes of their squadron. They are toasted at dinner. They sleep the satisfied sleep of a professional who has had a good day.

And two or three sorties later, they are dead.

This is not coincidence. This is not bad luck. This is a specific pattern, well-documented, predictable, and entirely preventable if a pilot is aware of it.

I call it the hangover of victory, and it has killed more pilots than any enemy weapon I know of.


## Why It Happens

The hangover of victory happens because success is poisonous to the habits that produced it.

A pilot who has a great mission has, by definition, done many things correctly. They flew with discipline. They managed their energy. They saw their targets before they saw them. They took shots they should have taken and refused shots they should have refused. They came home.

After the mission, in the relief and elation of their success, they do something that feels very natural: they relax.

Small relaxations. Nothing dramatic. Their scan becomes slightly less disciplined on the next sortie, because they are thinking about their recent victories and not about their current surroundings. Their energy management becomes slightly looser, because they have proved to themselves that they can win, and they do not feel the same urgency to fly every detail perfectly. They take a shot they would not have taken a week ago, because their confidence has grown.

Any one of these small relaxations is probably survivable. All of them together are not.

The cumulative effect, over two or three sorties after a major victory, is that the pilot has reverted to the kind of flying they were doing before they became competent. They have forgotten, in their success, why they were succeeding. And the next time they are in a tight engagement, the old sloppiness reasserts itself, and they pay the price they have taught themselves they do not need to pay.


## The Quiet Killer

I call complacency the quiet killer, because it does not announce itself.

Enemy fire announces itself. Mechanical failure announces itself. Even fear announces itself, by the physical symptoms it produces.

Complacency is silent. It creeps in. It feels, from the inside, like nothing. The pilot who has become complacent does not know they have become complacent. They feel exactly as they always have. They believe they are flying exactly as they always have. The changes are below the threshold of their own awareness.

The only way to fight the quiet killer is to actively, deliberately, look for it.

After every successful mission, and I mean every successful mission, you should sit down and review your flying with skepticism. Where did you relax? Where did you take shortcuts? Where did you rely on assumptions that had worked out this time but might not work out next time?

If you cannot find any such places, you are probably not looking hard enough. Every pilot cuts corners sometimes. The question is not whether you cut corners, you did, but whether you will catch them before they catch you.


## The Post-Victory Debrief

I want to describe a specific practice that has, over the course of my career, saved my life more times than I can count.

After every mission in which I killed an enemy, every single mission, I conducted a private debrief with myself.

Not the formal debrief with my squadron, which is also important and also mandatory. The private debrief that I did alone, usually the evening after the mission, in my quarters, with a cup of whatever was passing for coffee in that particular forward deployment.

In this private debrief, I asked myself three questions.

*One. Where was I lucky?*

Every combat kill involves some element of luck. The enemy pilot who was slightly less skilled than they should have been. The shot that hit the critical system instead of the armor plate. The wingman who was not where they needed to be at the critical moment. Every kill has these factors, and a pilot who does not acknowledge them is attributing to their own skill what was partly gift.

I identified the lucky factors. I named them. I wrote them down in a log book that I kept for this purpose. And I reminded myself, in specific terms, that those factors might not be present the next time.

*Two. Where did I make mistakes that got away with me?*

Even successful missions involve mistakes. A turn that was slightly wrong. A shot that was taken from a poor angle and hit by luck. A moment of target fixation that did not cost me because no one exploited it. These are the mistakes that are most dangerous, because they went unpunished, and a pilot’s brain rewards unpunished mistakes by repeating them.

I identified the mistakes. I wrote them down. I committed to doing better on the next sortie.

*Three. What habits did I demonstrate that I want to keep?*

Not every lesson of a successful mission is negative. Sometimes you do something well, and the reason you did it well is that you have built a good habit, and you want to reinforce that habit by noting it. I wrote down the things I had done right, so that I would remember to keep doing them.

These three questions, asked after every successful mission, kept me honest. They prevented the slow creep of complacency. They made my successes into opportunities for improvement rather than occasions for dangerous self-satisfaction.

I commend the practice to you. Do it your way, in your own words, with your own structure. But do it. The alternative is to let your successes train you to die.


## The Pilot Who Has Forgotten

I have known pilots who could not understand why their careers were in decline.

They were good pilots. They had been aces, or near-aces. They had medals. They had reputations. And then, somewhere in the middle of their careers, they started losing engagements they should have won. They started coming back from missions with more damage than they should have taken. They started making mistakes that, in earlier years, they would never have made.

Most of these pilots did not understand what was happening to them. They thought they were experiencing a run of bad luck, or a change in the enemy’s tactics, or some other external cause.

Almost always, the cause was internal. They had become complacent. Success had eroded the habits that produced success. They had forgotten the discipline that had made them who they were, and no one had told them they were forgetting.

Do not become that pilot.

Keep your discipline when you are winning. Keep your discipline especially when you are winning. The habits that got you to the top are the same habits you need to stay there, and the moment you stop practicing them is the moment you begin your decline.


## A Warning About Medals

Let me end this chapter with a specific warning about awards and medals.

I have received medals. I am not ashamed of them. They have their place.

But I have observed, over many years, that the period following a significant award is one of the most dangerous periods in a pilot’s career. Pilots who have just been decorated fly differently in the weeks after the ceremony. They fly with a sense of identity. They fly as “the pilot who won the Cross of Valor,” not as “a pilot doing their job on this particular sortie.”

The identity is the problem.

A pilot who is flying as an identity is not flying as a pilot. They are flying as a character in a story, the story of their own achievements, and the character sometimes takes actions that the pilot would not take. The character presses attacks that are not tactically sound. The character refuses to break off fights that should be broken off. The character takes risks because the character is supposed to be brave.

These risks are not, in the moment, felt as risks. They are felt as actions consistent with who the pilot is, which has been confirmed by the medal they have just received.

The medal, in this sense, is a weapon the service has unintentionally pointed at its own pilot.

When you are decorated, and if you do your job, you will be, remember that the decoration is a record of what you have already done. It is not a script for what you will do next. The next sortie is just the next sortie. The medal stays at home, in its box, where medals belong.

Fly the sortie. Not the medal.
