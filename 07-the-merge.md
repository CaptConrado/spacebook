_PART TWO: THE FIGHT ITSELF_

**CHAPTER FIVE**

# The Merge

THE merge is the moment when two ships, yours and the enemy’s, come into weapons range for the first time.

It is the most decisive moment in any engagement.

I mean that precisely. Not the most important moment, there are many important moments in a fight. I mean the most decisive. What happens in the first three to five seconds of the merge determines, more than any other single factor, how the rest of the engagement will unfold. The pilot who handles the merge well sets the terms of the fight. The pilot who handles it poorly spends the next two minutes trying to recover from a disadvantage that is, at best, difficult to overcome.

I have watched countless engagements, both as a participant and as a reviewer of combat footage, and I can tell you that most fights are decided at the merge.

The pilots who handle the merge well win. The pilots who do not, die.


## What Happens at the Merge

Before I tell you how to handle the merge, I need to tell you what the merge actually is, because most rookies have a fuzzy and romantic idea of it that bears little relationship to what happens in an actual combat engagement.

The merge is not a duel. It is not two knights jousting. It is not a stately exchange of fire as two pilots pass each other in the sky.

The merge is a blindingly fast geometric event in which two ships, approaching each other at a combined closure rate that may exceed several thousand meters per second, have a brief window, sometimes less than a second, in which they are within weapons range of each other. During this window, both pilots must simultaneously acquire their target, assess the geometry, make a tactical decision, and execute that decision.

All of this happens in the time it takes a normal person to blink.

The reason the merge is so decisive is that whatever happens in that blink cannot be undone. If you were in a good position at the merge, you are in a good position for the next phase of the fight. If you were in a bad position, you are in a bad position, and no amount of subsequent maneuvering can give you back the seconds you have already lost.


## The Three Questions

As you approach the merge, your mind must be asking three questions. Not in sequence. In parallel. Continuously, from the moment you first acquire the enemy ship on your sensors until the instant you pass each other.

The first question is: Where are they?

Where are they right now, right this instant, in relation to my ship? What is their altitude relative to mine? What is their closing angle? Are they coming straight at me, or are they coming at an angle that will give them a shooting opportunity I must defend against? You must have an accurate three-dimensional picture of the enemy’s position, and you must be updating that picture continuously.

The second question is: Where are they going?

Where will they be in three seconds? In five seconds? Are they holding their vector, or are they maneuvering? If they are maneuvering, which way are they pulling? A pilot who focuses only on where the enemy is right now, and not on where the enemy is going, is a pilot who will shoot at the enemy’s previous position and miss.

The third question is: What do they think I am going to do?

This is the question that separates the journeymen from the aces. The enemy is not a rock. They are a pilot, and they are making predictions about you just as you are making predictions about them. If you can figure out what they expect you to do, and then do something else, you will have an advantage. If you do exactly what they expect, they will be one step ahead of you for the entire merge.

Three questions. All at once. Every merge. No exceptions.


## The Decision

Based on the answers to those three questions, you must make one of four decisions, and you must make it in the opening seconds of the merge.

*Decision one: Shoot.*

If the geometry favors a shooting opportunity, if the enemy is passing through your guns at an angle and range that gives you a realistic firing solution, you shoot. You do not hesitate. You do not wait for a better shot, because there may not be a better shot. You take the shot you have, and you follow it through with discipline.

*Decision two: Turn.*

If the geometry does not favor a shot but does favor repositioning, you turn. You commit to the turn immediately, with full authority, and you try to come out of it in a position to either shoot on the next pass or to force the enemy into a disadvantageous energy state.

*Decision three: Extend.*

If the geometry is bad, if the enemy has the advantage and engaging them now would be suicidal, you extend. You accelerate away, using your full energy, to create separation, and you work the tactical situation to give yourself a better second merge on better terms. Extending is not running away. It is repositioning for a fight you can win.

*Decision four: Disengage.*

If the entire engagement has gone badly, if you are outnumbered, outmatched, out of energy, or out of time, you disengage. You break contact and you go home. There is no shame in this. A live pilot is an asset. A dead pilot is a waste. More on this in a later chapter.

The critical thing, for present purposes, is that you pick one of these four decisions, and you commit to it within the first two seconds of the merge.

Rookies die because they cannot decide. They hover between shooting and turning. They start to extend and then think better of it and try to turn, and in the middle of the turn they decide to disengage, and the enemy kills them in the middle of their third change of mind.

Pick a decision. Commit to it. Execute it.

You can always reassess on the next pass. You cannot reassess if you are dead.


## The First Shot

If your decision is to shoot, a word about the first shot.

The first shot of any engagement is the most important shot you will ever fire.

Not because it is more likely to hit than subsequent shots, in fact, it is often less likely, because you have had less time to set up the geometry. But because the first shot communicates something to your opponent. It communicates that you are a pilot who will take the shot. And that communication has a powerful psychological effect.

A pilot who knows their opponent will shoot at the first opportunity flies differently from a pilot who believes their opponent will hesitate. They fly more conservatively. They keep more distance. They give up angles that they would otherwise contest. They fight, in other words, at a disadvantage, because they are afraid.

Make your opponent afraid.

Take the first shot. Even if it is not a great shot. Even if it is a long shot. Even if it is a shot you know, in your heart, is probably going to miss.

Take it.

The shot establishes the terms of the engagement. It tells your opponent: you are now flying against a pilot who shoots. The next time you cross, I will shoot again. You must deal with that. You must plan around that. And while you are planning around my willingness to shoot, you are not planning your own offensive, and I have already won something, even if the first shot missed.


## The Merge Is Over

The merge lasts perhaps five seconds, start to finish. When it is over, you are either in a shooting position, a repositioning turn, an extension, or a disengagement.

Whatever you are doing, you are committed.

And whatever happens next, you handle, the way you were trained. With discipline. With economy. With the understanding that the fight is no longer a merge, it is something else, and the something else is the subject of the next chapter.
