**CHAPTER SEVENTEEN**

# What It Was All For

I HAVE come to the end of what I can teach you.

There is more, of course. There is always more. Every chapter of this book could be ten chapters, and each of those ten could be expanded into a book of its own. Combat flying is not a subject that can be exhausted. It can only be approximated, and I have given you my best approximation in the space I allowed myself.

But I want to close this book with something that is not instruction.

I want to tell you why I flew, and why, despite everything, I would do it again.


## The Reasons You Will Be Given

You will hear many reasons why you should fly combat.

You will be told that it is your duty to your nation, or your colony, or your service. You will be told that the enemy is evil and must be stopped. You will be told that you have been chosen, that you have a gift, that you will be a hero. You will be told about the traditions of the service, about the pilots who came before you, about the sacred trust you are being asked to take up.

Some of these reasons are true. Some of them are true in some circumstances and not in others. Some of them are lies, told by people who have never flown and who are comfortable sending other people to die for causes they will not serve themselves.

I am not going to tell you which is which. That is for you to figure out, over the course of your career, using your own judgment and your own values.

What I will tell you is that none of these reasons, not even the true ones, are sufficient to sustain a pilot through a long combat career. You need a reason that is yours. Not a reason that has been given to you. A reason that you have found, in your own life, for doing what you are doing.


## My Reason

I will tell you mine, for whatever it is worth.

I flew because it was the thing I was best at.

That is all. I was given a capacity for a certain kind of work, and the work was available, and I did the work. I did not fly for a flag, or for revenge, or for glory, or for any of the reasons the recruiting posters suggested. I flew because I was good at it, and because when I was doing it well, I felt, more than at any other time in my life, that I was being what I was supposed to be.

This is not a heroic reason. It is not the reason I would put in a speech to a graduating class of cadets. It is a small, private reason, and it would probably sound inadequate to most civilians.

But it is the reason that held up.

Over forty years. Over five campaigns. Over the losses of friends and wingmen and ships and many things besides. The big reasons I was given at the start, the patriotic ones, the moral ones, eroded over time, as I came to understand the complexity of the situations I was flying in. But the small private reason did not erode. I was good at the work. The work was there. I did the work.

If you find a reason like that, a reason that is specifically yours, not given to you by anyone else, hold onto it. It will sustain you when the public reasons do not.


## What It Adds Up To

At the end of a career in combat flying, you will have done many things.

You will have killed people. Let us not pretend otherwise. This is a profession in which killing is the central act, and no amount of euphemism about targets and kills and confirmations should be allowed to obscure that fact. You will have ended lives. You will have ended, among them, the lives of people who were themselves doing their jobs, who had families and hopes and histories, who were not personally responsible for whatever grand political dispute had brought them into conflict with you.

You will have to carry this.

You will also have saved people. Your wingmen, whom you covered. Your formation, whose enemies you killed before they could kill the members of your own group. The civilians of your home colony or station, whose defense was the ultimate purpose of your service. You will have saved more people than you can count, though you will never know most of their names, and they will never know yours.

You will have to carry this too.

You will have learned things that most people never learn. About yourself, about the nature of fear, about the limits of human capability, about what a person can do under pressure. These lessons are hard-won and they do not transfer easily to civilian life, but they are yours, and they are real.

You will have had friendships of a depth that is rarely possible in peacetime. The wingmen you flew with, the squadron mates you depended on, the commanders you served under, these relationships are not like ordinary relationships. They are forged in a fire that most people never encounter. The friendships that survive are deep in a way that is difficult to describe to those who have not been through it.

You will have lost more than most people lose.

And you will have loved more than most people love.

Whether it was worth it, on balance, that is a question only you can answer, and the answer may change over the course of your life. I have answered it different ways at different times. On the nights when I have been haunted by the faces of the dead, I have answered it one way. On the days when I have received letters from the children of the colonies I helped defend, I have answered it another.

What I can tell you is that the question is not a simple one, and anyone who answers it simply, in either direction, is probably not paying attention.


## The Final Words

I will close with the final words I was given when I finished my own flight training, by an instructor whose name I have never forgotten and whose face I can still see.

They said: “Fly well. Come home. Make the ones who loved you proud.”

I have thought about those three sentences for forty years. They have become, for me, the distilled essence of what a pilot is for. The first is the professional standard. The second is the personal discipline. The third is the reason.

Fly well, because anything less is a disservice to the people you serve and a waste of the training you have received.

Come home, because your life is not yours to throw away, however glorious the circumstances might seem.

Make the ones who loved you proud, because, in the end, this is what it is all for. Not the medals. Not the victories. Not the campaigns that will fade into history. The people who love you, who are waiting for you to come home, whose lives will be different for the rest of their days depending on whether you make it back or whether you do not.

Fly for them.

Fly well.

Come home.

That is the whole of it.

And that is the end of what I have to teach you.

Good luck out there. I believe in you, or I would not have bothered writing any of this down.

Now get out of my office and go fly.

Commodore Bobby “Mulligan” Thorne, Ret.
