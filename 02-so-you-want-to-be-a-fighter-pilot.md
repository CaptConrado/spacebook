**INTRODUCTION**

# So You Want to Be a Fighter Pilot

MOST of the "nuggets" I have trained in the past did not die because combat flying is hard.

Combat flying is hard, certainly. But it is not mysterious. It is not a secret art practiced by the favored few. It is a trade, and like any trade it has its tools and its techniques and its disciplines, and anyone of sound mind and body who is willing to apply themselves can become competent at it.

Most green pilots die because they make it complicated.

They read too many books by authors who never flew a mission. They watch too many training films produced by officers who have not sat in a cockpit since the last war. They listen to the stories of pilots who lived, and they forget that the pilots who didn’t live cannot tell their side. And they arrive at their first squadron convinced that the secret of combat flying lies in some maneuver, some trick, some clever piece of business that will mark them out as special.

Nothing of the kind is true.

The aces I have known, the men and women who lived long enough to become aces, which is not the same thing, were almost uniformly simple pilots. They did a small number of things and they did them well. They flew their ship the way it was meant to be flown. They shot when they had a shot and they did not shoot when they didn’t. They held position on their wingman and their wingman held position on them. They watched their instruments and they watched the imager and they watched their fuel. They did not try to be fancy, because the fancy pilots were dead.

The pilots who died in their first six sorties, and I want you to understand that is the overwhelming majority of combat losses, first-sortie losses, losses in the opening weeks of a pilot’s career, died almost without exception because they were trying to do too much.

They were trying to fly like the ace on the recruiting vids.

They were trying to perform maneuvers that they had practiced a grand number of two times in the simulator.

They were trying to impress their flight leader, or their squadron, or themselves.

They were trying, in short, to be good, when what they should have been trying to do was be competent. Competence keeps you alive long enough to become good. Nothing else does.

I have written this for the pilot who understands that and for the pilot who does not yet understand that but has the sense to listen.

If you are the sort of green pilot who believes that your natural gifts will carry you through, that your reflexes and your youth and your native cunning will be enough, please stop reading now. Go to your Wing Commander and ask to be transferred to ship operations. I cannot help you. Nobody can help you. The enemy and space will teach you what I cannot, and it will charge you considerably more.

For the rest of you: welcome.

I am going to tell you, as plainly as I know how, what forty-odd years of combat flying have taught me about staying alive and hitting what you aim at. I am going to tell you what you must do to build a foundation that will serve you in any ship, against any enemy, in any engagement. I am going to tell you what the great pilots do differently from the dead ones, which is less than you might think, and simpler than you would want to believe.

I am not going to tell you everything.

Nobody can tell you everything. The Void is too big and combat is too strange and every fight is different in its particulars, and any instructor who promises to give you a complete education in a book or a course or a lifetime is a liar.

What I can give you is the trunk of the tree.

The branches you will grow yourself, out in the dark, in engagements I will not be there for. If the trunk is sound, the branches will hold. If the trunk is rotten, no amount of careful branch-pruning will save the tree.

So. Let us begin with the trunk.
