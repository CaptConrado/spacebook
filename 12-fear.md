_PART THREE: THE THINGS NOBODY TEACHES YOU_

**CHAPTER TEN**

# Fear

I HAVE been waiting for this chapter with some trepidation.

Fear is a subject that combat instructors avoid, and there are reasons for the avoidance. A book of combat instruction is supposed to give the reader confidence. It is supposed to suggest that the job can be done, and done by the reader, with the right training and the right attitude. A chapter that dwells on fear might undermine that message. It might introduce doubts into a reader who, up to this point, had none.

I have thought about this, and I have concluded that I must write the chapter anyway.

The reason is that the pilots who do not talk honestly about fear are the pilots who are most vulnerable to it. A pilot who believes they should not feel fear, when they inevitably do feel fear, will interpret the feeling as a personal failing. They will try to suppress it. They will try to pretend it is not happening. They will try, in short, to fly as if the fear were not there.

This does not work. The fear is there. It does not go away because you have refused to acknowledge it. And a pilot who is suppressing unacknowledged fear is a pilot whose cognitive resources are being partially consumed by the suppression, and therefore a pilot who is not flying at their best.

A pilot who acknowledges their fear, who understands it, who knows what it does to their body and their mind, who has worked out the techniques by which it can be managed, is a pilot who flies better than the one who is in denial.

So. Let us talk about fear.


## What Fear Is

Fear is an ancient system in the human nervous system. It evolved hundreds of thousands of years before any human being ever strapped into a fighter cockpit, and it evolved to solve problems that have nothing to do with combat flying.

The fear system’s job is to detect threats and mobilize the body to deal with them. When the system detects a threat, it releases a cascade of chemicals, adrenaline, cortisol, others, that produce a specific set of physical responses. The heart beats faster. The breath quickens. The muscles tense. The pupils dilate. Blood is routed away from the digestive system and toward the limbs. The field of view narrows. The sense of time distorts.

All of this is very useful if your problem is that you have just been charged by a predator on the savannah, and your best option is to run or fight.

None of it is useful, and most of it is actively counterproductive, if your problem is that you are strapped into a fighter ship in the middle of a combat engagement and need to make precise, calm, rational decisions under time pressure.

The fear system does not know the difference. It responds the same way to any perceived threat, regardless of what response the situation actually requires. And the response it produces, the narrow field of view, the racing heart, the tunnel vision, is almost perfectly designed to make you a worse pilot than you are when you are calm.

This is the problem that every combat pilot must solve.


## What Fear Does to You

Let me be specific about the effects of fear on your flying, because the specifics matter.

Your field of view narrows. This is sometimes called perceptual narrowing or tunnel vision, and it is a real, measurable phenomenon. Under stress, your visual attention collapses onto whatever object your brain has identified as the primary threat. You stop scanning. You stop seeing the periphery. You become, effectively, one-eyed and blind to most of the dark.

Your sense of time distorts. Some pilots report that time seems to slow down. Others report the opposite, that whole seconds vanish from their memory, that they cannot reconstruct the sequence of events of an engagement. Either form of distortion makes it harder to fly well.

Your fine motor control degrades. Your hands tighten on the controls. Your touch becomes coarser. The delicate grip we discussed in Chapter One becomes, under stress, a death-grip. You over-correct. You oscillate. You fight the ship instead of flying it.

Your decision-making narrows. Under fear, your brain reverts to well-rehearsed patterns. You do what you have done before. If what you have done before is a habit of skilled combat flying, this is fine. If what you have done before is a habit of poor flying, or if the situation requires something you have not rehearsed, fear will push you toward the familiar response even when the familiar response is wrong.

Your communication degrades. Fearful pilots talk too much or not at all. They miss calls. They garble calls. They speak in half-sentences. Their wingmen cannot understand them, and they cannot understand their wingmen.

All of these effects are normal. They happen to everyone. They happened to me, and they happened to every ace I ever knew, and they will happen to you.

The question is not whether you will experience them. The question is what you will do about them.


## The Pilot Who Is Not Afraid

Before I tell you what to do about fear, I want to address a myth that young pilots cherish and that kills them in distressing numbers.

The myth is that there are pilots who are not afraid.

There are not.

There are pilots who hide it well. There are pilots who have managed it so effectively that they scarcely notice it themselves. There are pilots who have, through long experience, come to find the sensation familiar and therefore less disorienting. But there are no pilots who are actually not afraid in combat.

Anyone who claims to be fearless is lying to you. Very often, they are also lying to themselves, and the self-deception is the most dangerous part, because a pilot who believes they are not afraid does not develop the techniques for managing the fear they are nonetheless experiencing.

The aces I have known were not fearless. The aces I have known were men and women who were frequently terrified in combat and who had, over years of training and experience, developed such a thorough set of techniques for managing the terror that they could continue to fly effectively while it was happening.

That is the model. That is what you should be aiming for. Not fearlessness, which is impossible. But management, which is achievable.


## Managing Fear

Let me tell you what actually works.

Breathing. This is the oldest technique in the book, and the one most frequently dismissed as too simple to be useful. It is not too simple. It works, and the reason it works is that breathing is the one autonomic function over which you have conscious control. When you slow your breathing, deliberately, the fear system receives a signal that the threat is not acute, and it moderates its response. Your heart rate comes down. Your peripheral vision returns. Your hands loosen.

The technique is four in, four out. Inhale for a count of four. Exhale for a count of four. Not dramatically. Not so deeply that you are hyperventilating. Just steady, even, deliberate breathing, maintained throughout the engagement.

You can do this while you fly. You should do this while you fly. If you are not breathing deliberately during an engagement, you are almost certainly breathing shallowly, and shallow breathing feeds the fear response.

Verbalization. Narrate your actions, quietly, to yourself. Not over the comm channel, you will be a talker, and we have discussed the problems with talkers. But internally, or just under your breath. “Turning right. Energy good. Checking six. Wingman on my four.” The act of verbalizing forces your brain to engage its language centers, which are the parts of the brain that are most useful in combat and that the fear response tends to suppress.

Preparation. The fear response is weaker for situations you have anticipated than for situations you have not. This is why combat pilots train so extensively. Not because the training teaches you skills you did not already have, though it does, but because the training familiarizes your fear system with the kinds of situations you are going to face, and reduces the intensity of its response when those situations actually occur.

The implication is that you should visualize, before every sortie, the range of situations you might encounter. Not to the point of paranoia. Just enough that, if one of those situations does occur, your fear system can say, “I have seen this before,” and moderate its response accordingly.

Accepting the fear. This is the hardest technique and the most important. You must accept that you are afraid, in real time, while the fear is happening. You do not suppress it. You do not deny it. You acknowledge it, “yes, I am afraid”, and you continue to fly.

The acknowledgment defuses the fear in a way that suppression does not. Fear suppressed grows stronger. Fear acknowledged tends to lose some of its hold. You are telling your brain, in effect, “I have heard you. I know you are worried. I am dealing with it.” And your brain, strangely enough, will often quiet down when it knows it has been heard.


## The Fear Between Missions

One more word on fear before I close this chapter.

There is a specific kind of fear that is not the fear you feel during a mission, but the fear you feel before and after.

The fear before a mission is the worst, in my experience. You are in your quarters. You are getting dressed. You are eating a breakfast you do not want. And you are thinking, with terrible clarity, about what might happen in the next few hours. This is the fear that has killed more pilots’ careers, not their lives, their careers, than any in-cockpit fear I have ever encountered. It is the fear that causes pilots to report sick when they are not. It is the fear that causes pilots to request reassignment to non-combat duties. It is the fear that grinds good pilots down over the course of a campaign.

The fear before the mission cannot be managed the same way as the fear during the mission. Breathing exercises will not do much. Visualization sometimes makes it worse.

What works for the fear before the mission is routine.

Establish a pre-mission routine, and adhere to it rigidly. The same breakfast. The same preflight walkaround. The same checklist of conversations with your crew chief. The same order of putting on your flight gear. The same moment of silence in the cockpit before you hit the ignition.

The routine gives your fearful mind something to hold on to. It provides a sequence of small, manageable actions, each of which you know how to do, and the completion of each of which is a small victory that builds toward the larger action of flying the sortie.

I have known pilots who ate the same breakfast, the same specific meal, for three years of active combat because changing it was, for them, too much to contemplate. I am not saying you have to go that far. But I am saying that a rigid routine is protective. It will get you through the mornings when nothing else can.

The fear after the mission is different again. It is the fear that arrives when the adrenaline drains and you realize, retroactively, how close you came to dying. It is often delayed, sometimes by hours, sometimes by days. It frequently comes at night, when you are trying to sleep. It is the fear that accumulates, over a long career, and that can, if not managed, develop into a chronic condition that ends careers and damages lives.

For this fear, there is no technique that works reliably. What works is talking about it. Talking about it with other pilots, who have been through the same things. Talking about it with the medical officers of your service, who are trained to help. Talking about it, when you are very lucky, with a spouse or a friend who is willing to listen without judgment.

Do not carry the fear after the mission alone. Many pilots have tried, and many pilots have paid for it. The fear is heavier than you think, and you do not have to carry it by yourself.
