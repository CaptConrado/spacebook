**CHAPTER NINE**

# Breaking Contact

THERE will come a time in your career, if your career is a long one, and I hope it is, when you will need to break off a fight and run away.

I want to tell you, at the start of this chapter, that there is no shame in this.

I will say it twice because you will not believe it the first time. There is no shame in breaking off a fight and running away.

The young pilots reading this, and by young I mean not just young in years but young in combat, which can be a pilot of any age who has not yet accumulated the hard experience that cures the illusion, the young pilots will not want to hear this. They have been raised on stories of pilots who fought to the last drop of fuel and the last round of ammunition. They have been shown training films that celebrate aggression and initiative. They have absorbed, without anyone needing to tell them directly, the idea that a real pilot presses the attack and a lesser pilot retreats.

This is a false idea. It has killed more promising young pilots than the enemy ever has.

The real pilots, the pilots who live long enough to have careers, know when to break contact. They know when a fight has turned against them. They know when their energy state is too poor to continue. They know when they are outnumbered or outmatched or out of position. And when they know these things, they leave.

They leave not because they are cowards but because they are professionals. They leave because they understand that a live pilot is an asset to their service, and a dead pilot is not. They leave because they understand that the mission is not to die nobly. The mission is to accomplish the objective and come home.

If breaking contact is what the situation requires, breaking contact is what a professional pilot does.


## When to Break Contact

There are specific tactical situations in which breaking contact is the correct decision, and I want to enumerate them, because rookies frequently fail to recognize them.

When you are out of ammunition. A fighter with no rounds left is a liability to their wingman and to themselves. They cannot contribute to the engagement. They can only occupy a position in the formation and hope the enemy does not notice them. If you are out of ammunition and the engagement is not already decisively won, you break contact and you go home.

When your ship is significantly damaged. A damaged ship has reduced performance. Reduced performance means reduced survivability. A pilot who continues to fight in a damaged ship is betting their life that they can compensate for the damage through skill, and over the long run, that is a losing bet. If your ship has taken meaningful damage, you break contact.

When you are out of energy. We have discussed the energy bank at length in earlier chapters. A pilot whose bank is empty cannot turn, cannot climb, cannot maneuver with any authority. They are a coast in progress, and their opponent will kill them in due course. When your energy is gone and cannot be replenished in the current engagement, you break contact.

When you are alone and outnumbered. Two against one is manageable if your wingman is still with you. Two against one with no wingman is not manageable. It is a fight you will lose, not because you are a bad pilot but because the geometry is against you. Break contact. Live to fight another day.

When the mission has been accomplished. This is the one that rookies most often miss. If you have done what you came to do, killed the target, completed the escort, delivered the ordnance, you do not continue fighting for its own sake. You go home. The point of combat is to accomplish missions, not to accumulate kills.

When the mission has become unaccomplishable. If the mission cannot be completed, staying in the fight serves no purpose. You report the failure, you disengage, and you get back to base so that a new plan can be developed.

In any of these situations, breaking contact is not a defeat. It is a tactical decision made by a competent pilot on the basis of accurate information.

Make the decision. Execute it. Do not second-guess yourself.


## How to Break Contact

Breaking contact is a skill. It is not just “running away”, if you simply turn and run, you will be shot in the back, and you will die on the way home. Breaking contact is a maneuver, and like all maneuvers, it must be done properly.

The fundamental principle of breaking contact is this: you must create enough separation from the enemy that they cannot engage you further, and you must do so before the enemy can close the gap.

There are several ways to do this, and the right one depends on the specific tactical situation.

The extension. You accelerate to maximum speed on a vector away from the enemy. This is the simplest form of breaking contact, and it works when you have an energy advantage, when your ship is faster than the enemy’s, or when you have more fuel available, or when you are already at a higher energy state. You simply outrun them.

The dive. If the enemy is above you or at your altitude, you can sometimes use a dive to generate separation. A dive converts altitude into velocity. You pick up speed you could not otherwise generate, and by the time you pull out of the dive, you may be out of the enemy’s weapons envelope.

The vertical extension. Conversely, if the enemy is below you and has committed to pursuing you, you can sometimes extend upward, climbing into a regime where their ship performs poorly and yours performs well. This works especially against enemies whose ships are less thrust-efficient at high altitudes.

The terrain mask. If there is a planetary body, an asteroid field, the hulk of a dead ship, or any significant feature within reach, you can put it between yourself and the enemy to break their hold on you. But understand what you are really doing, because in this it is easy to think like a flier of the old atmosphere and die of it. Their sensors do not see you the way an eye sees you. They see your drive, your heat, the wash of your emissions; they are sharp against a ship that is burning and nearly blind against a ship that is cold. The feature that masks you buys you a window, perhaps thirty seconds, and the way to spend that window is not merely to change your vector. It is to go cold. Cut the drive, shed what heat you can, shut down everything that radiates, and let your own momentum carry you out along a line they cannot predict. A ship that breaks line of sight and keeps burning has only moved the problem. A ship that breaks line of sight and goes cold has, for as long as its nerve and its momentum hold, simply ceased to exist as far as the enemy's image is concerned. That is the difference between a feature you hid behind and an escape you actually made.

The wingman sacrifice. I list this reluctantly because it is not something to be done lightly. If you and your wingman are both trying to break contact and only one of you can, one of you must commit to holding the enemy’s attention while the other extracts. This is a desperate measure, used only when neither pilot can escape alone, and it generally results in the loss of the wingman who holds the enemy’s attention. It should never be the lead’s decision to make for the wing; it is a decision the wingman must make for themselves, voluntarily, knowing what it costs. I have seen it done. I have seen it done well. I hope you never have to do it, and I hope, still more, that you never have to watch it done for you.


## The Disengagement

Breaking contact is not complete when you have generated separation. Breaking contact is complete when you have returned to a safe position, either rejoined the formation, returned to the base, or reached a point at which you are no longer a target.

The period between generating separation and reaching safety is the most dangerous part of the entire disengagement, and rookies frequently relax too soon.

Understand this. An enemy who has just failed to kill you is now highly motivated to kill you. They know where you have gone. They have your last vector. They may well be following you.

Do not assume you are safe simply because you can no longer see them on your sensors. Assume they are pursuing you until you have confirmed otherwise. Maintain your scan. Maintain your evasive flying. Do not fly a straight line for any extended period, a straight line is a prediction, and a prediction is an invitation.

When you are close enough to your base or your formation that you have friendly support, you can relax. Not before. Relax too early and you will arrive at your home base with a fighter on your tail, and you will die in sight of the hangars.


## The Shame That Is Not Shame

I want to close this chapter by returning to the theme I opened with.

There will be pilots in your squadron, possibly even commanders, who will judge a pilot for breaking contact. They will call them cautious. They will call them weak. They will imply, without quite saying it, that a real pilot would have pressed the fight.

Do not listen to these pilots.

The pilots who speak this way are almost always pilots who have not been in a position themselves where breaking contact was the correct decision. They are either very young and very lucky, or they are staff officers who have not flown combat in years. Their opinion on the matter is worth precisely nothing.

The pilots whose opinions matter are the pilots who have been there. The pilots who have broken contact themselves, or who wish they had, or who lost wingmen who did not. These pilots understand, in the way that only direct experience can teach, that breaking contact at the right moment is a skill, and that it is the skill of a professional, and that it should be neither apologized for nor boasted about.

You broke contact. You came home. You will fly again tomorrow.

That is the job.

That has always been the job.

Anyone who tells you otherwise has not yet understood what combat flying is actually about.
