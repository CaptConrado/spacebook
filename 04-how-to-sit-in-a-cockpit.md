**CHAPTER TWO**

# How to Sit in a Cockpit

IT may seem absurd to devote a chapter to the question of how to sit in a chair.

Any fool knows how to sit in a chair. We have all been doing it since we were old enough to be propped up at the family table. A cockpit seat is not, in its essentials, very different from any other chair, and the act of sitting down in it is not an act that should require three thousand words of instruction from a retired commodore.

And yet.

I have watched more pilots kill themselves through bad posture than through bad gunnery, bad tactics, and bad luck combined.

I will say that again, because I can see you rolling your eyes.

More pilots die from how they sit in the cockpit than from any of the glamorous, dramatic failures you have been taught to worry about. They die because they cannot pull a hard turn without graying out. They die because they cannot reach the controls they need to reach when they need to reach them. They die because their heads are jammed against the canopy and they cannot see. They die because their harness is loose and their body is slamming around inside the cockpit during maneuver, and by the time the fight is over they have broken ribs or a dislocated shoulder and they are flying home with one hand and half a brain.

All of this is preventable.

All of it is preventable in the thirty seconds before you hit the ignition, if you have been properly taught how to sit in the cockpit and if you have the discipline to do it every single time.


## The Posture

Sit all the way back in the seat. Your spine against the seat back. Your shoulders against the shoulder pads. Your head, when relaxed, resting against the headrest.

This sounds obvious. It is not. I have watched pilots, grown men and women, sit perched on the front edge of their seats like children at a schoolroom desk, leaning forward toward the controls as if they could fly the ship better by getting closer to it. They cannot. What they can do, under high-g maneuvers, is launch themselves face-first into the instrument panel.

Sit back.

Your back is straight but not rigid. There is a small hollow in the lower back, your lumbar curve, and the seat should support it. If the seat does not support it, you adjust the seat. If the seat cannot be adjusted to support it, you complain to the maintenance chief until the seat is replaced. A seat that does not support your lower back will, over the course of a long sortie, destroy your ability to turn your head and move your arms with any freedom, and you will come home a passenger in your own ship.

Your shoulders are back and down. Not hunched. Not raised up toward your ears, which is what most pilots do when they are nervous. Shoulders down. Chest open. The ship should not have to fight your own body to turn your head.

Your head is up. Your chin is level. You are looking forward and slightly up, into the image, not down toward where the old pilots kept their panels. A pilot whose natural resting gaze drops low is a pilot who is going to be hit from above before they ever know what killed them.


## The Feet

Your feet are on the rudder pedals.

Both feet. At all times. Flat against the pedals, not perched on the balls of your feet, not tucked underneath, not resting on the cockpit floor. Your feet are part of the control system, and they need to be where the control system needs them to be, at all times.

A pilot whose feet wander off the pedals during a long cruise is a pilot who is going to have to scramble them back into position when the shooting starts, and that scramble is going to cost them the fight.

Put your feet on the pedals when you climb in. Leave them there until you climb out.


## The Harness

The harness is not a seatbelt. It is not there to keep you comfortable. It is there to keep you glued to the seat so that the seat can protect you.

A properly adjusted harness is tight enough that you cannot slip a finger between the strap and your body. Not tight enough to cut off your circulation, but tight enough that you are, in a real sense, bolted to the ship. When the ship maneuvers, you maneuver with it. When the ship pulls nine g’s, you pull nine g’s, and your organs do not slide around inside your torso.

Most rookies, and a distressing number of veterans, wear their harnesses loose.

They do this because a tight harness is uncomfortable on long flights. This is true. A tight harness is uncomfortable. A loose harness is worse than uncomfortable, it is a death sentence in any fight that involves real maneuver.

Cinch your harness before every sortie.

Cinch it tighter than you think you need.

If you come home and your shoulders are bruised, good. You did it right.


## Head Position

A fighter pilot sees with their head, not their eyes. Let me explain what I mean.

Your eyes can only take in part of what surrounds you at once, and the synthesized image, for all that it wraps you on every side, is only useful in the directions you are actually attending to. The six o'clock position, directly behind you, is the most famous and the most deadly, because that is where you will be killed from, nine times out of ten. But your attention has blind spots above, below, to either side, and in the quarters between. You cannot watch a sector you are not turned toward. You must turn your head, and let the image turn with it.

Turning your head, in a cockpit, is a whole-body exercise.

You turn your head by first turning your shoulders, not far, just enough to allow the neck to rotate freely. You tilt your chin. You use your peripheral vision as you rotate. And critically, you rotate slowly enough to actually see what you are looking at, and fast enough to cover the full scan before something sneaks up on you.

A pilot whose head is locked forward is a pilot who is already dead. They just have not been told yet.

You must practice the head-turn until it is automatic. You must do it on the ground, in your quarters, in the simulator, before you ever take a live ship up. You must do it until your neck muscles are conditioned and until the motion feels natural. And then you must do it every few seconds for the entire duration of every sortie you ever fly, because the second you stop is the second you die.


## The Ship in Your Body

There is a part of sitting in a cockpit that the old pilots never had to learn, and it is the most important part, so I have saved it for last.

Your helmet reads you. There is a sensor in it that listens to your nervous system, and after the long business of training and the longer business of flying, it comes to know your intentions the way a good horse knows its rider. You do not operate this ship the way the ancients operated theirs, with a hand for every switch and a bank of dials for every system. You fly with the stick and the throttle, and very nearly everything else, the weapons, the sensors, the channels you talk on, the fine adjustments, you call for with a thought or a word, and the ship answers. This is why the cockpit can be sealed and quiet and nearly bare. The controls are mostly inside you.

The ship talks back the same way. The four things that will kill you if you stop attending to them, your fuel, your heat, the temperature of your weapons, and the rounds you have left, are given to you three ways at once. There is a screen, if you want to read a number. There is a figure laid into the image, if you want to glance at it. And there is the thing underneath both, the sensation the helmet feeds directly into your body, so that you know your fuel is low the way you know you are hungry, and you feel your weapons running hot the way you feel a fever coming on. You do not read these. You feel them.

A young pilot has the sensation but has not yet learned to trust it. They feel the warmth of a hot gun and have to stop and think what it means, the way a child sounds out a word before they know it. By the time they have translated the feeling into a fact and the fact into a decision, the moment is gone. A veteran does not translate. Their body simply knows, and acts, and is on to the next thing before the rookie has finished reading the screen. This is most of what we mean when we say a pilot has hours. The hours are in the body. They cannot be put there by a book, including this one. They can only be flown in.

Understand one hard thing about this, and carry it with you. The helmet reads what is actually in you. It does not read the pilot you wish you were. If what is in you is calm, it gives the ship calm. If what is in you is panic, it gives the ship panic, faster and more faithfully than you could have managed with your hands. The interface does not save you from yourself. It is a clearer mirror than most people ever have to look into, and it shows the ship exactly what is there. The whole of the discipline in this book is, in one way or another, the work of making sure that what is there is worth showing it.


## The Pre-flight Sit-down

Before you hit the ignition, sit in the cockpit for thirty seconds with your eyes closed.

I mean this literally. Sit. Eyes closed. Breathe.

Feel the seat behind you. Feel the harness across your chest and shoulders. Feel the rudder pedals under your feet. Feel the stick in your right hand and the throttle in your left. Run through the physical inventory of your body in the cockpit, piece by piece, and make sure everything is where it needs to be.

Only when you have done this should you open your eyes and begin the flight.

I know this sounds superstitious. It is not. It is the thirty seconds in which you are making the mental transition from a person who sits in a cockpit to a person who flies a ship. It is the thirty seconds in which your body is calibrating itself to its instruments. It is the thirty seconds that separates a sortie from a tragedy.

Every ace I have ever known does something like this. They do not all call it by the same name, and some of them would deny they do it at all. But watch them in the cockpit before a mission. Watch their hands settle onto the controls. Watch their shoulders relax back into the seat. Watch the way their breathing slows.

That is the ritual. That is the transition. That is how you sit in a cockpit.

Everything else is just details.
