**CHAPTER EIGHT**

# The Wingman

I WANT to tell you a story before I begin this chapter proper.

Late in my career, in the Meridian campaign, I was shot down over the moon of Meridian Prime. My ship, an F-7 Hammer I had flown for years and trusted past the point of good sense, had been hit in the engines by a long-range torpedo, a shot I never saw coming because I was, at that moment, engaged with a fighter group that had dropped on us from a high angle and was pressing me hard.

I was out of the fight in the first sixty seconds. My engines were dead. My maneuvering thrusters were leaking. I was coasting on a bad vector, and three enemy fighters had peeled off from the main engagement to finish me.

I was going to die.

I knew I was going to die. I had been flying long enough to know a dead ship when I was sitting in one, and I was sitting in one. I remember thinking, very calmly, that it had been a good run and that I was sorry I would not see my sister again.

And then one of my pilots, a Lieutenant named Elena Vasquez, twenty-four years old, less than a year in combat, dove her ship between me and the three of them at a closure rate that should have killed her outright. She fired everything she had. She killed two of them. The third she forced off in a defensive split. She came around for a second pass while I coasted on a dead ship, and she drew their fire for the four minutes it took my beacon to bring a rescue up from the fleet. By the time the rescue arrived she had killed a fourth and her own hull was venting atmosphere from seven separate breaches.

She lived. I lived.

I owe her my life. I want to be precise about the size of that debt, because it is larger than the single afternoon over Meridian Prime suggests. Vasquez was not in the habit of saving only me. She did it again and again across her career, for pilot after pilot, going into the place no sane flier would go because someone was in it who needed bringing out. It is why she has so many friends among people who do not, as a rule, make friends easily, the senior officers of three fleets who are alive because a quiet Lieutenant from New Jalisco decided their lives were worth more than her own margin. In the end it cost her. She went in one time too many, took an injury that ended her flying, and was put on the ground for good while she was still, by any measure of skill, the best pilot I ever knew. She is alive as I write this. She does not fly anymore. She is the one who asked me to write this book, and when Elena Vasquez asks you for something, you have already spent forty years learning that the only answer is yes.

I am telling you this story because I want you to understand, from the first sentence of this chapter, that there is no more important person in your professional life than your wingman.

Not your commanding officer. Not your maintenance chief. Not your flight instructor, who is writing this book. Your wingman.

Everything that follows in this chapter is an attempt to explain why that is, and what it means for how you should fly.


## What a Wingman Is

A wingman is the other pilot in your two-ship formation.

This is the simple, mechanical answer. It is not wrong. But it is like saying that a spouse is the other person on your marriage certificate, technically correct, wildly inadequate to the actual meaning of the thing.

Your wingman is the pilot whose job it is to keep you alive while you do your job. And your job, in turn, is to keep your wingman alive while they do theirs.

This is not a matter of policy or procedure. It is the fundamental architecture of combat aviation. A single fighter in combat is a vulnerable thing, visible from every direction, coverable from every angle, defensible from none. Two fighters, flying in proper formation, are not twice as capable as a single fighter. They are something more than that, because each pilot’s eyes are watching the sectors the other pilot cannot see, and each pilot’s guns are available to cover the other pilot’s vulnerabilities.

A two-ship formation, well flown, is in some sense a single four-dimensional organism, two ships, two pilots, one tactical unit.

A two-ship formation, poorly flown, is two individual ships in loose proximity, each one fighting alone and wondering why the other is not helping.

The difference is in the discipline of the flying. And the discipline of the flying is what this chapter is about.


## The Lead and the Wing

In a two-ship formation, one pilot is the lead and the other is the wing.

These are not rank designations. They are role designations, and they can shift between pilots over the course of a career depending on who is senior, who has more experience, and who is better suited to lead on a particular mission.

The lead's job is to fly the mission. They are the one making the tactical decisions about the engagement, where to go, when to engage, when to disengage, what targets to prioritize. They fly the offensive pattern. They take the shots.

The wing's job is to protect the lead. They fly in a position that covers the lead's six and provides mutual support. They call out threats the lead cannot see. They engage defensively to protect the lead when the lead is committed to an attack.

Note what the wing's job is not.

The wing's job is not to fly their own fight. The wing's job is not to rack up their own kills. The wing's job is not to prove themselves to the rest of the squadron.

The wing’s job is to protect the lead.

Rookies almost always get this wrong. A rookie wingman, the first time they engage, will see an enemy ship presenting a shooting opportunity, and they will peel off from their lead to take the shot. They may well hit it. They may well get a kill.

They will probably get their lead killed in the process, because the moment they peeled off, the lead's six was uncovered, and whatever enemy was setting up to exploit that vulnerability, and there is always one, will have done so.

The first rule of the wingman, and the rule you must hold before you ever fly as wing, is this: you do not leave your lead.

You do not leave them to take a shot. You do not leave them because the engagement has drifted and you think you see a better opportunity elsewhere. You do not leave them because you are bored or restless or feeling heroic.

You stay on their wing. You cover their six. That is your job. That is the only job you have. If you do that job and nothing else, you will have done enough, and your lead will come home, and you will come home, and the mission will have been a success.


## The Calls

A wingman's voice on the channel is as important as their guns.

I want to talk for a moment about the calls, the short, standardized words that wingmen use to coordinate with their leads.

There is a vocabulary for this, and it differs slightly between services and eras, but the core calls are universal.

“Break left” or “break right.” This is the call your lead makes when they see a threat at your six and you must turn hard to evade it. You do not ask why. You do not hesitate. You break in the direction called, at once, with everything the ship has. Your lead has seen something you have not, and the call is the only thing between you and a round in your back.

“Tally.” This means you have the target. You say it to confirm, for your lead, that you hold whatever they are looking at in your own image. Short. Clear. Certain.

“No joy.” This means you do not have it. Equally short, equally clear. If your lead calls a contact and you cannot find it in your image, you say so. You do not pretend to a tally you do not have, because in a sky full of phantoms the difference between a contact your lead can see and one only you can see may be the difference between a real ship and a ghost.

“Bandit, two o'clock high.” A threat confirmed hostile, with bearing and elevation, given relative to your own nose, never to anything outside the ship. A contact not yet identified is a bogey; a contact known to be hostile is a bandit; the words are not interchangeable and the channel is no place to be loose with them.

"Engaged." You are in a fight. Your lead knows you are not available for other tasks until you call otherwise.

"Clear." The threat is neutralized or departed. You can be re-tasked.

These calls are short because the comm channel must not be clogged. Every second of unnecessary chatter is a second during which an actual emergency call cannot be heard. Be brief. Be clear. Say only what must be said, and say it only once.

The worst wingmen I have flown with were the talkers. They narrated the engagement. They commented on the quality of the other side's flying. They offered tactical suggestions no one had asked for. They filled the channel with noise, and when a real crisis came, no one could hear the call that would have saved a life.

Do not be a talker. Be a wingman.


## Cover and Be Covered

The fundamental tactical principle of two-ship flying is mutual support.

You cover your wingman. They cover you. Neither of you is ever alone, as long as you are flying in proper formation.

This sounds simple. In practice it takes constant discipline. In the heat of a fight your instinct will be to fly your own fight, to chase your own opportunities, to answer your own threats, to fly as if you were alone in the dark.

You are not alone. You are half of a unit, and the unit’s survival depends on you doing your half of the work.

This means that when your wingman calls a threat, you answer. When your wingman maneuvers, you anticipate the maneuver and hold your support position. When your wingman commits to a shot, you cover their six for the length of the shot, and you do not take your attention off their tail until the shot is made and the geometry has resolved.

And when you are the one maneuvering, you trust that your wingman is doing the same for you. You do not second-guess. You do not hold back out of worry that no one is watching your back. You fly with the confidence of a pilot who knows their six is covered, because it is covered, because their wingman is doing the job.

If you ever have reason to believe your wingman is not doing the job, if you have flown with a pilot who peels off, who does not call threats, who cannot be trusted to be where they are meant to be, you do not fly with them again if you can help it. You take it to your squadron commander. You recommend them for more training, or for a role that does not rest on mutual trust.

Do not cover for a bad wingman. A bad wingman will kill you in the end, and your good opinion of them will not matter to anyone once they have.


## The Partnership

If you are fortunate, over the course of your career, you will fly with a few wingmen with whom you develop a genuine partnership.

This is not a matter of friendship, though friendship often follows. It is a matter of flying chemistry. You find a pilot whose instincts complement yours, whose reflexes match yours, whose judgment under fire is aligned with yours. When you fly with that pilot, the two-ship formation becomes something more than the sum of its parts. You do not need to speak on the comm channel, because you can read each other’s maneuvers. You do not need to call threats, because you are both seeing the same sky in the same way.

These partnerships are rare. Guard them when you find them. Volunteer for missions together. Request each other when you can. Build the trust that will, in time, save both your lives.

Elena Vasquez and I were never formally paired. She was a Lieutenant in a wing I commanded, and I was a Group Captain who had no business flying as anyone's wingman by then. But in the stretch of the Meridian campaign that we flew the same sky, we had the thing I am describing. I could keep my attention on the fight because I knew where her attention was. She could commit because she knew I would cover the way out. The chemistry does not care about rank, and it does not last forever, and you should recognize it and be grateful for it on the days you have it.

I have told you she went in once too many and lost her flying to it. I have not told you that I was not there that day either, that the pilot whose life she was saving was not me, that I learned of it the way I have learned of most of the important things in my life, after the fact, from someone else, too far away to have done anything. I thought for a long time about whether I might have changed it had I been her wingman. I thought about it for weeks.

The truth is that I could not have changed it. It was not a failure of wingmanship, hers or anyone's. She did the thing she always did, and this time the dark took its price instead of letting her walk away again. Sometimes the best pilot in the sky pays in full for doing exactly the right thing. You must make your peace with that, or this trade will break you.

But I will tell you the thing I most want you to carry out of this chapter, and I mean it as deeply as anything in this book. The worst weight a wingman can bear is the knowledge that they failed the pilot off their own wing. Guard against earning that weight. Do your job. Fly the way you were taught. Cover your lead. Cover your wing. Do not make yourself the reason a good pilot did not come home. I have been spared that particular weight, and I was spared it by Elena Vasquez, who covered me when it was my turn to be the one who did not come home, and who is alive today to be thanked, which is more than most of us in this trade are ever given the chance to say.
