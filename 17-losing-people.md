**CHAPTER FIFTEEN**

# Losing People

THIS will be a short chapter.

There is not much that I can tell you about losing people that will help you. The losing, when it comes, is unlike anything that can be prepared for, and the techniques I will describe are inadequate to the thing they address.

But I must write the chapter anyway, because you will lose people in this career, and you deserve to be warned.


## You Will Lose Friends

In a career of any length in combat aviation, you will lose friends.

You will lose some of them to combat. You will lose some of them to accidents. You will lose a few of them to suicide, which is a subject no one talks about honestly but which accounts for a sobering percentage of combat-pilot mortality, particularly in the years after major campaigns end.

You will lose more of them than you expect. The statistics are there, and they are bleak, but statistics do not prepare you for the specific fact that your particular friends will be represented in them. You will think, when you are young, that you and your squadron mates are somehow exempt. You will think, each time you lose one, that it was bad luck and that it will not happen again. And then it will happen again.

There is no technique for not losing friends. There is only the fact of the losing, and the question of how you will carry it.


## The First One

The first one is the hardest.

I do not mean that the subsequent ones are easy. I mean that the first loss is the loss that teaches you that this is going to happen, and that you are going to have to find a way to keep flying anyway. Every loss after the first is added to the burden you are already carrying, but the first loss is the moment when you begin carrying the burden at all.

My first loss was a man named Ingram, and to call him my first loss is already to misdescribe him, because he was my friend before he was anything that could be lost in a war. We went through flight school together. He had been a fixture of my life for more than twenty years by the time the Ceres campaign killed him, one of the last people alive who had known me before the rank, before any of it. We had taken different paths out of training. I went to fighters; he went to electronic warfare, the quiet, technical, crewed side of the service, flying the craft that fill the enemy's image with ghosts and strip the ghosts out of ours. He was the most careful pilot I ever knew. It did not save him.

He was killed on a long patrol into the asteroid field, not in combat, not in any dramatic circumstance, but in a freak failure of his craft's life support. He was unconscious before anyone aboard knew there was a problem. And it was not only Ingram. An electronic warfare craft carries a crew, three or four souls working the sensors and the deception together, and the malfunction did not distinguish among them. It took all of them. The ship flew itself home on autopilot, because there was no one left alive to fly it, and it beaconed its return as if nothing were wrong, and the ground crews went out to meet a routine recovery. I was on the deck when they opened it. I will not tell you what we found. I will only say that there are deaths that teach you something and deaths that teach you nothing, and the second kind are the ones that stay.

I remember every detail of the hours after I learned. I remember the exact light in the briefing room. I remember the words the squadron commander used. I remember not being able to feel my feet. I also remember flying a mission the next morning, because the service does not stop for grief, and the campaign does not pause for a pilot's adjustment. You will learn that a friend is dead, and then you will be strapped into a cockpit six or eight or twelve hours later, and you will have to fly. Nothing prepares you for this.

Ingram had a fiancee, a teacher, back on his home colony. I told her myself. I owed him that, and I owed her that, and it was the hardest thing I did in the whole of the Ceres campaign, harder than any of the flying. She moved on, in time, as she should have, and built a life that he was meant to be in and was not. I am glad of it. I stopped keeping up with her because keeping up with her hurt more than I could justify, and I will not dress that up as anything nobler than it was. She did the brave thing, which was to live. I did the easier thing, which was to look away. I have turned into enemy fire I was less afraid of than I was of her letters.

That is the shape of the first one. A careful man, a freak failure, a crew gone with him, a life on a colony somewhere rearranged around his absence, and a friend left to fly the next morning. There was no lesson in it. That is what made it the first one, and the hardest.


## What Does Not Work

I have watched pilots try various strategies for dealing with the losses, and I want to tell you which ones do not work.

Suppression does not work. The pilot who tries to push the loss out of their mind, to pretend it is not affecting them, will find that it affects them anyway, more destructively than if they had acknowledged it. Suppressed grief becomes physical illness, impaired judgment, broken relationships. It finds a way to come out, and the ways it finds are worse than the ways you would have found if you had let it out on your own terms.

Cynicism does not work. Some pilots, after losses, adopt a protective cynicism. They pretend to not care. They make grim jokes. They tell new arrivals not to bother learning their names because they will be dead soon. This is a defense mechanism, and in the short term it feels like relief. In the long term, it hollows the pilot out. The cynic who has stopped caring has stopped being a person who can fly with a wingman, and the job requires that they can.

Heroism does not work. Some pilots, after losses, respond by becoming more aggressive, pushing attacks harder, taking greater risks, as if they could avenge the dead by adding more kills to their tally. This is perhaps the most dangerous response of all. The aggression is not tactical. It is emotional. It leads to mistakes. The pilot who is flying to avenge their friend is the pilot who is going to join their friend.


## What Does Work, A Little

What works, imperfectly, is some combination of the following.

Talking. Talking to other pilots who have been through the same thing. Talking to the medical officers who are trained to help. Talking to anyone in your life who is willing to listen. Do not carry the loss alone. You cannot carry it alone. Even if the person you talk to has nothing useful to say, the act of saying the thing out loud is part of the process.

Ritual. Many squadrons develop rituals around their losses. Empty chair at the next squadron dinner. Toast at the end of the next briefing. Name spoken aloud before the next mission. These rituals serve a real purpose. They acknowledge the loss. They provide a form in which grief can be expressed. They connect the loss to the continuity of the squadron, which is a continuity the dead pilot was part of and is still, in memory, part of.

Participate in your squadron’s rituals, whatever they are. If your squadron does not have them, consider creating them.

Time. This is the most honest thing I can tell you, and the least useful. Time does not heal. Anyone who tells you time heals has not lost enough people. But time dulls. The losses do not hurt less, but they hurt in ways you can live with after enough time has passed.

The immediate grief, the acute grief of the first week after a loss, becomes over time a quieter sorrow that you can carry while still flying, still eating, still sleeping. You will not forget the pilots you have lost. You should not forget them. But you will learn to live with the fact of having lost them, and the learning is a function of time and nothing else.


## The Debt

I want to end this chapter with a thought that I have found, over my career, to be the most useful single idea I have encountered on this subject.

The pilots you lose are pilots who, in some sense, taught you something.

The dead wingman showed you what you should have been doing differently. The dead flight lead showed you what kind of commander you do or do not want to be. The dead ace showed you both the highest heights of the profession and the final limit of what skill alone can accomplish.

You owe them something, for what they taught you.

The debt can never be repaid. The pilots are dead. But you can acknowledge the debt by flying in a way that honors what they gave you. You can take the lessons they purchased with their lives and apply those lessons in your own flying, and in your own instruction of younger pilots, and in the way you carry yourself as a professional.

This is the only memorial that means anything, in the end. Not the statues at service academies. Not the names on the campaign monuments. The memorial that matters is the flying of the pilots who are still alive, shaped by the lessons of the ones who are not.

Carry the dead well. It is the last thing you can do for them, and it is not nothing.
