**CHAPTER FOUR**

# Throttle Discipline

I HAVE already told you that your left hand, on the throttle, is never still. I want now to go deeper into what that means, because throttle discipline is, in my estimation, the single most underappreciated skill in all of combat aviation, and the one that most reliably separates the pilots who survive their first year from the pilots who do not.

Throttle discipline is not a glamorous subject.

It does not make for good training films. It does not feature in the heroic stories that get told in the ready room after a successful mission. It does not have a dramatic name. It is not the sort of thing that a young pilot, reading their first combat manual, is likely to pay much attention to.

And yet.

I will tell you, as a man who has studied the logs and the recordings of more dead pilots than I care to count, that if you could go back and fix one thing about the flying of the average combat casualty, it would not be their marksmanship or their maneuvering or their tactics. It would be their throttle hand.

The dead pilots almost all burned too hard, too early, and too long.

By the time they needed their reserves, they had none. By the time they needed energy, their energy was spent. By the time they needed to climb out of a bad situation, their engines were running lean and their tanks were running dry, and they died because their left hand had been lazy.


## Fuel Is Time

Let us begin with a simple truth that every pilot knows intellectually but few pilots feel in their bones.

Fuel is time.

Every pound of reaction mass in your tanks represents a certain number of seconds of powered maneuver. When you are burning at military power, you are spending those seconds quickly. When you are burning at cruise, you are spending them slowly. When you are coasting, unpowered, you are not spending them at all.

In combat, the pilot with more time has an advantage over the pilot with less. It is as simple as that.

The pilot who has fuel can reposition. They can climb. They can burn for a new angle. They can choose to disengage and re-engage on their own terms. They can wait out their opponent’s fuel state and strike when the opponent has run themselves dry.

The pilot who is out of fuel cannot do any of these things. They can only continue on their current vector, and they can only continue on it for as long as their momentum carries them, and then they are a target.

Combat engagements are frequently decided not by the pilot who shoots the best, but by the pilot who manages their fuel the best. You will hear veterans say this and nod their heads, and then you will watch them firewall their throttles at the opening merge and burn half their fuel in the first ninety seconds.

Do not be that veteran.


## The Gears of the Throttle

Think of your throttle not as a continuous slider, but as a set of gears. Each gear has its purpose. Each gear has its cost.

There is idle. This is where you sit on the pad, pre-flight. This is where you coast when you have no demand on your engines. Fuel consumption is near zero. Thrust is near zero. A ship at idle is a ship that is saving every drop of reaction mass for when it is needed.

There is cruise. This is the long-haul setting, the economical burn, the throttle position you use for most of the sortie between engagements. Fuel consumption is moderate. Thrust is sufficient to maintain course and make modest corrections. A good pilot spends most of their sortie at cruise.

There is combat power. This is the setting for engagement. It is the throttle position that gives you the energy you need for tight turns and fast repositioning. Fuel consumption is heavy. You cannot sustain combat power indefinitely, at most, perhaps twenty minutes, depending on your ship and your reserves. Combat power is expensive, but it is necessary, and the pilot who refuses to use it when they need it is going to be beaten by pilots who do.

And there is military power, sometimes called burnout or firewall. This is the throttle against the stop, the engines giving everything they have, propellant pouring through at the maximum rate the ship will allow. Military power is your sprint setting. You use it for seconds, not minutes, and not only because of what it costs you in fuel. Held too long, it cooks you. The engines and the hull shed heat only so fast, and past a certain point the ship crosses into the redline where the structure itself begins to suffer, where plating warps and seams complain and the airframe quietly spends some of the life it was built with. There is no hard wall that stops you. The ship will let you ruin it. That is precisely why military power demands discipline: you use it to close a critical gap, to break a critical threat, to buy a critical margin, and then you bring it back to combat power or cruise the moment the crisis passes, before the heat and the strain start taking back more than the burst was worth.

The rookie's habit, always, is to spend too much time in the higher gears. They fire up their engines at military power on takeoff and never come down. They engage at military power and stay there through the whole fight. They run home at military power because they are rattled, and they land on fumes.

The ace’s habit, by contrast, is to spend as much time as possible in the lower gears. They cruise home when they can, not because they are lazy, but because every second they spend cruising is a second of fuel they have not spent, and that fuel is a reserve they can draw on in the next engagement.

An ace, on approach to a fight, will often coast for long stretches, engines at idle, relying on momentum, conserving everything. They will not burn until they need to burn. And when they do burn, they burn precisely as much as the situation requires, and not a pound more.


## The Energy Bank

Here is the mental model I have used my whole career, and I commend it to you.

Your ship has a bank account. The currency is energy. Every pound of fuel in your tanks is a deposit. Every meter per second of velocity is a deposit. Every meter of altitude is a deposit. Your combat potential, at any moment, is the total balance of that account.

Every maneuver you perform withdraws from the account. A tight turn costs energy. A hard climb costs energy. A full-throttle burn costs energy faster than anything else.

Every moment of coast, every straight-line cruise, every dive that converts altitude into velocity, every moment of idle, is a deposit, or at least a moment in which you are not making a withdrawal.

A combat engagement, viewed through this lens, is a race to bankrupt the other pilot before they bankrupt you. If you can force them to spend their energy faster than they can replace it, you will win. If they can force you to do the same, they will win. The pilot with the healthier bank account at any given moment has the advantage.

The implications of this model are profound, and most rookies do not fully grasp them until they have been in combat for some time.

It means that you do not engage on your opponent’s terms. If you can force your opponent to burn harder than you, you should.

It means that you do not turn when you do not have to turn. Every turn is a withdrawal.

It means that you do not climb when you do not have to climb. Every climb is a withdrawal.

It means that you spend your energy on the engagements you can win, and you conserve it on the engagements you cannot afford to waste it on.

And above all, it means that you pay attention to your throttle, constantly, consciously, with the full engagement of your flying mind, because the throttle is the tap on the fuel bank, and the fuel bank is the source of all your options.


## The Discipline of the Left Hand

Let me end this chapter with what I consider the most important piece of advice I can give a young pilot on the subject of throttle discipline.

Your left hand must be as trained as your right hand.

Most pilots pour enormous effort into training their stick hand. They practice turns and rolls and yanks and pulls. They develop a fine touch on the stick, a feel for the ship, a vocabulary of inputs that is instantly responsive to any demand.

And then they leave their throttle hand lazy. They let it sit at cruise for hours. They firewall it in combat. They return it to cruise for the ride home. They use three or four throttle settings in a whole sortie, and they think they are doing a good job.

You must train your left hand like you train your right.

When you are in the simulator, practice pulsing the throttle. Practice small adjustments, five percent up, five percent down. Practice pairing throttle movements with stick movements, so that the two hands are always in concert. Practice coasting. Practice coming off the throttle entirely during a maneuver and re-engaging it at the moment of maximum need.

When you are in the real ship, do the same. Never let your left hand fall asleep. Never let it sit idle at any setting for more than a few seconds. Keep it moving. Keep it engaged. Keep it part of the conversation.

If you do this, you will find, not immediately, but over the course of months and years, that your flying has become substantially more efficient, more precise, and more survivable. You will find that you are arriving at engagements with more energy than your opponents. You will find that you are outlasting pilots who, on paper, are faster and more maneuverable than you are.

You will find, in short, that you have joined the ranks of the pilots who understand that the left hand is not a passenger. It is a partner.

And you will start to live longer than the pilots who have not yet figured it out.
