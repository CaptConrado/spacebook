**CHAPTER THREE**

# Reading the Image

A fighter pilot has two jobs.

The first job is to find the enemy before the enemy finds them.

The second job is everything else.

If you do the first job well, the second job becomes manageable. If you do the first job poorly, nothing you do in the second job matters, because you will not live long enough for it to matter. The pilot who finds the enemy first usually shoots first, and the pilot who shoots first usually wins. It is nearly that simple.

And yet, despite its simplicity, the business of seeing in a fighter cockpit is the single most neglected discipline in combat aviation. It is neglected in flight school, where the curriculum is crowded with systems knowledge and navigation and procedures, and the art of the lookout is given perhaps a single lecture. It is neglected in squadron training, where the pressure to produce a pilot who can fly the ship and shoot the gun crowds out the quieter disciplines. It is neglected in the cockpit itself, where the pilot's instinct, again and again, is to trust the picture in front of them without ever asking how that picture was made.

Understand what you are actually looking at. You sit sealed inside your hull, the shields up, no window on the dark at all. What you see is not the battle. What you see is the synthesis, the image your ship assembles from its sensors and projects around you, a constructed world that wraps you on every side and feels, if the engineers have done their work, exactly like looking through glass that is not there. It is the finest instrument ever built for a pilot. It is also a story your ship is telling you about the dark, and like any story, it can be wrong. The pilot who forgets this dies believing the last thing the image told them.


## The Scan

A fighter pilot does not look at things. They scan.

There is a difference, and it is critical.

Looking at something means fixing your gaze on a point and holding it there until you have gathered whatever information you meant to gather. This is how human beings naturally use their eyes, and it is a habit that works perfectly well in ordinary life, where the things we need to see tend to stay in one place.

Scanning means moving your gaze continuously across your field of view, pausing briefly at each point to absorb what is there, and then moving on. It is the opposite of how we use our eyes on the ground. It is a trained skill. It does not come naturally and it must be practiced until it does.

The scan pattern I was taught, and the one I have taught for forty years, is the box. You divide the image into a grid, left, center, right, high, low, and you move your attention through the grid in a regular rhythm. Left-center-right-high-low-center. And repeat. And repeat. And repeat.

Not so fast that you take in nothing. Not so slow that you cover only a fraction of the volume around you. A rhythm. A cadence. An unceasing, disciplined sweep of your attention across the entirety of what the synthesis is showing you, and a part of your mind held back to ask, always, whether what it is showing you is true.

In combat, the cadence tightens. In cruise, it loosens. But it never stops.

A pilot whose attention is fixed is a pilot who is blind.


## Target Fixation

Target fixation is the name we give to the phenomenon of a pilot locking their eyes onto their target and being unable to look away.

It happens to new pilots constantly. It happens to veterans occasionally. It has killed more aces than any single enemy weapon in the history of combat flying.

Here is how it goes. A pilot engages an enemy ship. They close. They align their guns. They are, in their own mind, about to make the kill. And in the final seconds of their approach, all of their attention, every bit of their cognitive bandwidth, every scrap of their peripheral awareness, every inch of their field of view, narrows down onto the silhouette of the enemy in their gunsight.

They do not see the second enemy coming in from their five o’clock.

They do not see the rock they are about to fly into.

They do not see the wingman of the ship they are about to shoot, sliding in on their blind side for the kill.

They see their target, and they see nothing else, and in the half-second before they fire, they are shot down by something they never looked at.

This is target fixation.

The defense against it is not complicated, but it requires discipline, because it requires you to do something your instincts will be screaming at you not to do. The defense is this: in the moments before you fire, you must force your eyes off the target.

You look left. You look right. You check your six. You look up. You look down. You do all of this in the final seconds of the approach, when every fiber of your being is demanding that you stare at the thing you are about to kill.

And then you look back at the target, and you fire.

This will feel wrong. It will feel like you are wasting time. It will feel like you are giving the enemy a chance to evade, and sometimes you will be doing exactly that, and the enemy will evade, and you will have to come around for another pass.

That is acceptable.

What is not acceptable is being so fixed on your target that you fail to notice the pilot who is about to kill you.

A kill is a kill. Next pass. The pilot who dies in the middle of making a kill is not a hero. They are a corpse with a final guncam recording, and the recording will be shown to nuggets in flight school as an example of how not to die.

Do not be that recording.


## The Dead Man's Stare

There is a related phenomenon, less dramatic than target fixation but in some ways more dangerous, which we call the dead man’s stare.

This is what happens to a pilot whose eyes have simply stopped working.

They are tired. The mission has been long. They have been flying for three or four hours, and their scan has gradually degraded from a disciplined sweep into an unfocused gaze, and finally into nothing at all. Their eyes are open. They are looking forward. But they are not seeing anything. Their brain has stopped processing visual information. They are, effectively, flying blind in the open dark.

The dead man’s stare kills pilots in the final hour of long sorties, on the way home, when the fight should be over and they have let themselves go soft.

The enemy knows this.

The enemy will wait for the tired pilots, the stragglers, the ones who think they are safe because they are heading back to base. And they will fall on them from the one direction the fatigued pilot has stopped watching.

The defense against the dead man’s stare is to know that it happens, and to catch yourself when it is happening, and to physically reset your eyes.

The physical reset is simple. You close your eyes for one full second. Then you open them and deliberately refocus, first on something near, the edge of a console at arm's length, then on something the image places far away, a distant contact or a body in the dark. Near, then far. Near, then far. This re-engages the focusing muscles in your eyes, which become fatigued and lazy over long flights, and it forces your brain to start processing what you are seeing again.

You should do this every ten minutes on a long sortie. Every five minutes if you are tired. And every single time you notice that you cannot remember what you have been looking at for the last thirty seconds.


## The Image Lies

Let me now tell you the most important thing in this chapter, and the thing that the old books, written for pilots who flew with their own eyes on the real dark, could never have told you.

The image lies. Not always. Not even often. But enough that the pilot who trusts it without reservation is a pilot living on borrowed time. The enemy spends a great deal of effort making your image lie to you, and the dark itself will do the same for free.

Consider the phantom. The other side carries electronic warfare craft whose whole purpose is to feed your sensors contacts that are not there, ships of pure fiction that your synthesis will paint as solidly as the real ones, sitting in your image at a range and bearing you can read off as plainly as truth. Fire a missile at a phantom and you have spent one of the few you carry on nothing. Turn to fight a phantom and you have given your back to the real ship that the phantom was sent to hide. Our own electronic warfare crews do the same to them, and a hard engagement can fill the volume around you with more ghosts than men. Learning to feel which contacts are real, by the way they move, by what the rest of the picture is doing, by the small wrongnesses that no amount of fakery quite erases, is not a thing I can teach you in a book. It is a thing you will learn in the dark, slowly, and the price of the early lessons is high.

Consider the dark itself. Radiation, the debris of old fighting, the wash of a nearby star, a cloud of dust that should mean nothing, any of these can thin your image, smear it, fill it with noise, or quietly subtract a contact that is really there. The pilot who picks the ground of the fight is choosing, among other things, what their enemy's image will and will not show. The old hands read the weather of the dark the way a sailor reads the sky, and they fly accordingly.

So the rule I was given by old “Blacksmith” Moray, in the early Rigel years, was not the rule the old books taught. It was harder. Trust the image enough to act on it, he said, and never so much that it can surprise you. A pilot who will not act until they are certain is already dead, because certainty does not come in time. A pilot who acts on the image as if it were the truth itself is dead the first time it lies. You must live in the narrow place between, believing and doubting at once, and you must do it for every second of every sortie.

There is a final defense, and I give it to you knowing that you should almost never use it. Your shields can be retracted. In harbor we drop them as a matter of course, to fly home by eye, and a cadet comes to think of the bare window as a thing for safe places only. It is not only for safe places.

There was a moment over the rocks at Ceres where my image showed me three contacts off my high quarter, close, and I did not believe them. The picture was too clean. The geometry was too kind. I retracted my forward shield in the middle of the engagement, for less time than it takes to say it, and I looked at the dark with my own eyes, the way my father's generation looked at everything. There was nothing there. The image had been lying to me, fed a story by an enemy electronic warfare craft I never saw. I brought the shield back up, turned into the contacts that were real, and lived. The two pilots who had been sent to die while their ghosts kept me busy did not.

I do not recommend retracting your shields in combat. With the shield down, anything that reaches your forward hull reaches you, and the dark is full of things that reach. I tell you the story only so that you understand the principle beneath it, which is the whole of this chapter and very nearly the whole of this book.

The image is the finest tool you will ever be given, and it is not the truth.

Learn the difference, and verify, and trust your own eyes against the picture the day the picture has earned your distrust.
