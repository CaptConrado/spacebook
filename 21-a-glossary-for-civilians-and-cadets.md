**APPENDIX B**

# A Glossary for Civilians and Cadets

THIS glossary is for the reader who has picked up this book without a thorough background in the vocabulary of combat aviation. I have tried to define terms as I use them in the text, which is not necessarily the same as the official doctrinal definitions that various services employ. The usage is mine. Argue with me at your peril.

Ace. Traditionally, a pilot with five or more confirmed kills in combat. In practice, the threshold varies by service and era, and the title carries more weight in some cultures than in others. I use the term in its traditional sense.

Bank account, energy. A mental model in which a ship’s combat potential is understood as the sum of its velocity, altitude (in a gravity well), and fuel. See Chapter 4.

Bogey. An unidentified contact. Not yet confirmed as hostile, but treated with appropriate caution until identified.

Break. A hard, immediate turn, usually executed defensively. “Break left” and “break right” are wingman calls directing the lead to turn hard in a specified direction to avoid an incoming threat.

Burn. To run the engines at a power setting that consumes fuel for thrust. “Burning” implies active engine use, as opposed to coasting on momentum.

Canopy. The transparent dome over the pilot’s cockpit. Used in harbor when the blast shields are retracted; in combat the shields are up and the pilot flies by the image.

Clear. A wingman call indicating that a specified threat is no longer present or no longer a concern.

Closure. The rate at which two ships are approaching each other. High closure rates are characteristic of head-on merges; low closure rates are characteristic of tail chases.

Commit. To make a tactical decision and follow through on it. A pilot who has committed to an attack is a pilot who is no longer considering alternatives and is executing on the chosen course.

Cruise. A moderate throttle setting suitable for long-range travel without engagement. Also used as a verb.

Deflection shot. A shot taken at a target crossing the shooter’s line of fire at an angle, requiring lead calculation. See Chapter 7.

Disengage. To break contact with an enemy and leave the area of engagement. See Chapter 9.

Energy state. The total combat potential of a ship at a given moment, considering its velocity, altitude (in a gravity well), and fuel reserves.

Engaged. A pilot is engaged when they are actively committed to a combat action that requires their full attention. A wingman who calls “engaged” is telling their lead that they are not available for other tasks.

Extension. A maneuver in which a pilot accelerates away from an engagement to generate separation, usually for repositioning purposes.

Firewall. To push the throttle to maximum. Also: the throttle setting itself.

Flight. A tactical formation of two to four ships flying together. A two-ship flight is the basic unit of combat aviation.

Formation. The arrangement of ships in a flight, and by extension, the discipline of flying in those arrangements.

g, g-force, g-loading. A unit of acceleration equal to the gravitational pull of a standard planet at its surface. Fighter pilots routinely experience loads of several times this value during maneuvers. See Chapter 2 and Chapter 10 for the implications of high g-loading on pilot performance.

Gunnery. The art and science of aiming and firing a ship’s weapons.

Hit. A confirmed connection between your projectiles and the enemy’s ship. May or may not result in a kill.

Instantaneous turn rate. The fastest rate at which a ship can change heading at a given moment, using maximum available maneuvering thrust. Not sustainable for more than a few seconds. See Chapter 6.

Joy, no joy. “Joy” means visual contact with a specified target. “No joy” means no visual contact. Standard wingman calls.

Kill. A confirmed shootdown of an enemy ship. The standard by which combat success is traditionally measured, though not the most important standard.

Lead. Two meanings. First, the lead pilot in a formation, the pilot making tactical decisions for the flight. Second, the amount a pilot aims ahead of a moving target to account for projectile travel time.

Merge. The moment when two ships enter weapons range of each other. See Chapter 5.

Military power. The maximum throttle setting the engines can produce. Fuel-expensive, sustainable only briefly. Also called firewall or burnout.

Mulligan. Originally a golf term for a second attempt at a bad shot. In my case, also a callsign, awarded after a crash-landing in training that I should not have survived. I was given a new ship and the squadron said I had taken my mulligan on life. I do not know whether I have used it up yet.

Off, on. Short for “offline” and “online.” A system that is “off” is not functioning; a system that is “on” is functioning.

Pad. The docking station where a ship is parked when not in use.

Position, positional advantage. The geometric relationship between two ships during an engagement. Good position is a position from which you can shoot without being shot at; bad position is the reverse.

Reversal. A maneuver in which both pilots in an engagement change direction, typically to bring guns to bear on each other. See Chapter 6.

Scan. A disciplined, rhythmic sweep of the pilot’s visual attention across the full field of view. See Chapter 3.

Six, six o'clock. The position directly behind the ship. “Check your six” means “look behind you.”

Sortie. A single combat mission, from takeoff to landing.

Stick. The primary flight control, held in the pilot’s right hand, which directs the ship’s pitch and roll.

Sustained turn rate. The fastest rate at which a ship can change heading while maintaining its energy state indefinitely. Slower than the instantaneous turn rate, but usable for extended engagement. See Chapter 6.

Tactical display. The cockpit instrument that shows the positions of known contacts in the ship’s sensor envelope. Useful, but not a substitute for the image.

Tally. Visual contact with a specified target. See “joy.”

Target fixation. The cognitive error of focusing so intently on a target that one loses awareness of one’s surroundings. A common and deadly rookie error. See Chapter 3.

Throttle. The primary power control, held in the pilot’s left hand, which adjusts the engine output. See Chapter 4.

Tracking. Keeping one’s guns aligned with a moving target, particularly during a turn. See Chapter 7.

Vertical, the. The third dimension of maneuvering, upward and downward, as opposed to the horizontal plane. Meaningful inside a gravity well; in deep space, all directions are equivalent. See Chapter 6.

Wing, wingman. The pilot flying in the supporting position in a two-ship formation. See Chapter 8.
