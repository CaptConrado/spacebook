**CHAPTER TWELVE**

# Knowing Your Ship

I WANT to begin this chapter with a hard statement.

If you do not know your ship, you have no business flying it in combat.

I do not mean knowing it in the abstract. I do not mean knowing its specifications from a performance manual, or its silhouette from a recognition card, or its combat history from a squadron archive.

I mean knowing it in the way that a horseman knows their horse. I mean knowing how it handles at different altitudes and different speeds and different fuel states. I mean knowing the sound it makes when the engines are stressed, and the way the airframe vibrates when you approach the edge of its envelope. I mean knowing where the rivets are, and which panels your crew chief has replaced recently, and which instruments tend to drift and which are reliable.

I mean knowing the ship the way you know your own body.

A pilot who does not know their ship is flying a stranger. They are trusting their life to a piece of machinery they have not bothered to understand. And when the ship behaves in ways that they have not anticipated, as ships always do, eventually, they will be surprised, and their surprise will kill them.

Do not be surprised by your own ship.

Know it.


## Walking the Hull

The first discipline of knowing your ship is the walkaround.

Every sortie, every time, without exception, you walk the hull before you climb in. You look at every panel. You look at every control surface. You look at every weapons mount. You look at the thrusters, the sensor housings, the seal of the cockpit you are about to be sealed inside. And you look at the armor, because the armor tells a story the logbook does not. Polarized ablative plate wears as it works; it sheds itself to save you, and a ship that has been in hard fighting carries its history on its skin. A crew chief replaces it section by section, and you learn to read which quarters are fresh and which are down to the structural layer, because the worn quarter is the one you do not want to present to the next pilot who means to kill you.

You are looking for anomalies, anything that is different from what you saw on the last walkaround. A panel that was tight is now slightly loose. A small stain of hydraulic fluid that was not there before. A hairline crack that might be new, or might just be the light. A weapon that is not properly seated in its rack.

Most of the time, you will find nothing. That is fine. The point of the walkaround is not to find problems, it is to be in a state where, if a problem exists, you will notice it.

A pilot who does not walk around their ship is trusting that someone else has done so, and that the someone else has caught everything. The someone else is usually your crew chief, who is competent and professional. But your crew chief is taking care of many ships, and they are tired, and they are human, and sometimes they miss things.

The walkaround is your last chance to catch the thing they missed.


## Knowing Your Crew Chief

This brings me to a subject that is not usually discussed in combat instruction, but that I consider fundamental.

Your crew chief is the second most important person in your professional life, after your wingman.

They are the person who keeps your ship flying. Every system that works when you press its button, the ignition, the guns, the comms, the sensors, the life support, works because your crew chief made it work. Every ship that comes back damaged from a mission is made flyable again by your crew chief. Every upgrade and retrofit that improves your combat capability is installed by your crew chief.

You owe your crew chief everything.

I have seen pilots who did not understand this. They treated their crew chiefs as servants. They shouted at them when the ship had problems. They blamed them when missions went badly. They took their work for granted.

These pilots died at rates significantly above average.

I say this with some confidence because I have looked at the data, and the data is clear. Pilots who had good relationships with their ground crews had ships that worked when they needed them to. Pilots who did not have good relationships with their ground crews had ships that developed mysterious and unfortunate problems at critical moments.

I am not suggesting that crew chiefs deliberately sabotaged pilots they disliked. I am not cynical enough to believe that, and I do not think it is true. What I am saying is that a crew chief who likes a pilot will go the extra mile, will stay an hour later to double-check a questionable panel, will advocate for the ship to be pulled from rotation when it needs deeper maintenance, will communicate concerns promptly and clearly. A crew chief who does not like a pilot will do their job, and nothing more, and in combat aviation the margin between doing one’s job and going the extra mile is often the margin between life and death.

Know your crew chief. Know their name. Know their family. Know how they take their coffee. Ask them about their work. Listen when they tell you things. Thank them, genuinely, when your ship comes back.

This is not about being nice for its own sake, though that is a good reason on its own. It is about being a professional pilot who understands that their ship is a team effort, and that the team extends beyond the cockpit.

And there is a deeper reason, which I will put plainly though the regulations would prefer I did not. Every senior pilot trims. The ship is delivered to you with its computer tuned for the average flier, cautious by design, quick to warn, quick to hold you back from the edges of what the airframe can do. You feel its caution in your body, because the ship speaks its state to you through the helmet, the fuel and the heat and the strain arriving as sensation as much as number, and the default profile is calibrated to make you flinch a little early. As you learn the ship, you trim that caution back, a little at a time, until the warnings come when you want them and not before. It is against the rules. The senior officers who write the rules trimmed their own ships when they were squadron leaders, and they know it, and they look the other way, because the rule exists to protect the pilot who has not yet earned the right to ignore it and to be ignored by the pilot who has. But you cannot trim a ship alone. The crew chief knows your profile. The crew chief maintains it, defends it, hides it from the inspections that would flag it, and tells you the truth when your trim has drifted past sense into danger. They could kill you with an afternoon's quiet work on a panel and you would never know until you were in the dark with the enemy. You trust them with your life every single time you climb in. That kind of trust is not issued with the ship. It is built, one sortie at a time, over years, and it is the most important thing in this chapter.


## Flying the Envelope

To know your ship, you must fly it at every part of its performance envelope.

This does not mean that you should be performing dangerous experimental maneuvers in combat. It means that in training, in practice sorties, in the simulator, you should be pushing the ship to see how it behaves in every regime.

How does it handle at maximum altitude? At minimum altitude? At maximum speed? At minimum controllable speed? At high g-loading? At low fuel states? With one engine out? With weapons loaded versus unloaded? With sensors degraded?

All of these conditions may arise in combat. Some of them may arise suddenly and without warning. And if the first time you experience them is in the middle of a fight, you will not have the time or the attention to learn how the ship behaves. You will be struggling with an unfamiliar ship while also trying to fight. And the fight will go poorly.

Take the time, in peace, to know your ship in all its moods.

Some of this knowledge is specific to the individual ship. Two ships of the same class, built on the same production line, delivered to the same squadron, will behave slightly differently. This one pulls slightly to the left in hard turns. That one has an engine that runs a bit rich. The other one has a sensor that drifts two degrees over the course of a long sortie.

You learn these individual quirks by flying the specific ship you are assigned. You do not learn them by reading the manual. The manual describes the ship as it was designed. Your ship is not the designed ship; it is the built ship, modified by the ship it has become through months or years of flying and maintenance.

Know your specific ship. Not just the class.


## The Rituals of the Ship

I want to mention, briefly, the rituals that pilots develop with their ships.

Some pilots name their ships. Some pilots paint them with personal markings. Some pilots have specific sequences of pre-flight actions, touching certain panels, speaking certain words, completing a private checklist that has nothing to do with the official checklist.

I do not want to encourage superstition. A pilot who believes they will die if they do not perform their ritual is a pilot who has placed their confidence in the wrong thing.

But I also want to acknowledge that the rituals serve a purpose, and the purpose is real.

The rituals create a relationship between the pilot and the ship. They transform the ship from a piece of assigned equipment into something the pilot has an investment in. They make the ship their, in a psychological sense, even when it is technically the property of the service.

A pilot who has that relationship with their ship will fly the ship better. Not because the rituals have magical power, but because the pilot who cares about their ship is the pilot who pays attention to it, who notices its quirks, who treats it with the respect that a complex machine deserves.

Develop your relationship with your ship. Use rituals if rituals help you. Do not let the rituals become a substitute for actual competence, but do not dismiss them either.

The ship is your partner in combat. Treat it that way.
