**CHAPTER SIXTEEN**

# Growing Old in a Young Person's Game

COMBAT flying is a young person’s profession.

I want to say this plainly, at the start of this chapter, because some of my older readers will want to argue with me about it, and I want to set the terms of the argument before we begin.

The raw physical attributes that combat flying requires, reflexes, g-tolerance, visual acuity, endurance, peak in most human beings somewhere in their early twenties and decline gradually from there. By the time a pilot is in their late thirties, measurable degradation has occurred. By the time they are in their fifties, the degradation is substantial. No amount of training or conditioning can entirely prevent it. It is biology, and biology does not negotiate.

This does not mean that older pilots cannot fly combat. Many older pilots have flown combat superbly. I flew combat into my fifties, and I was considered, I am not being falsely modest, a useful combat asset throughout my career.

But I did not fly the same way at fifty that I flew at twenty-five. I could not have. And if I had tried to fly the same way at fifty that I flew at twenty-five, I would have been dead at forty.

This chapter is about how to age as a pilot. About how to adjust your flying as your body changes. About how to remain useful, indeed, how to become more useful in some ways, even as the raw physical advantages of youth slip away.

It is also about how to know when to stop.


## What Declines

Let me be specific about what changes as a pilot ages.

Reflexes. The speed at which your nervous system processes information and produces a motor response decreases steadily from your mid-twenties. The decline is gradual, a percent or two a year, but it is cumulative, and by your forties it is significant. The snap-shot you could make at twenty-five requires a little more thought at forty-five.

G-tolerance. Your cardiovascular system’s ability to maintain consciousness under high sustained acceleration declines with age. This is partly reversible with conditioning, but only partly. An older pilot will gray out or black out at g-loads that a younger pilot could sustain comfortably.

Visual acuity. Your eyes change. Near vision tends to go first. Far vision and color perception follow. Night vision degrades. You can compensate to some degree with corrective lenses and with increased visual discipline, but the raw capability is not what it was.

Endurance. Long sorties become harder. The fatigue after a long flight is more severe and takes longer to recover from. You cannot fly as many sorties in a row as you once could.

All of these are real and measurable. None of them can be entirely overcome.


## What Improves

But some things improve with age, and these are the things that can, if you let them, make up for what you have lost.

Judgment. Your ability to read a tactical situation, to anticipate enemy actions, to know when to engage and when to break off, all of these deepen with experience. The older pilot makes better decisions than the younger pilot, and good decisions are often worth more than fast reflexes.

Patience. You learn to wait for the shot instead of taking the shot that is merely available. You learn to let engagements develop instead of forcing them. You learn to conserve energy and fuel and ammunition for the moments when they are really needed. A patient pilot beats an impatient one, even when the impatient one has better raw skills.

Pattern recognition. After enough combat, you begin to see patterns that younger pilots cannot see. You recognize the shape of an engagement before it has fully developed. You anticipate enemy tactics because you have seen them before, in different forms, over many years.

Composure. You have been in situations that should have killed you. You have come through them. The sheer experience of survival accumulates, and it produces a kind of calm that is difficult to fake and impossible to teach. Under stress, the older pilot flies more smoothly, not less, because they have been through worse.

These are the assets of the older pilot. They are real, and they are valuable, and they can sustain a combat career well into the years when raw physical capability has declined significantly.

But they cannot sustain a career indefinitely. And the pilot who thinks otherwise is courting disaster.


## Adjusting Your Style

The aging pilot must adjust their style.

This is not optional. A pilot who flew aggressive knife-fight engagements in their twenties cannot continue flying them in their forties. Their reflexes will not support it. They will lose the close-range contests they used to win. And the losses will come suddenly, because the decline is gradual enough that the pilot does not feel it happening until a specific engagement reveals that they are no longer the pilot they were.

The adjustment, ideally, happens before the catastrophic loss. The aging pilot notices that their snap-shots are no longer as clean, that they are winning close engagements more narrowly than before, that they are coming home with more damage than they used to take. They do not wait until one of those engagements ends in a shot-down ship. They adjust proactively.

The adjustment is usually toward patience. The older pilot stops trying to win the fast fights and starts trying to win the slow fights. They use position instead of speed. They use judgment instead of reflexes. They use the geometry, the energy bank, the long perspective, all the assets of the experienced pilot.

They become, in effect, a different kind of pilot than they were when they were young. The same ship, the same training, but a different pilot, because they are a different person.

This is how you age as a combat pilot. You change with the changes in your body. You adjust your style to your capabilities.


## Knowing When to Stop

And eventually, eventually, you stop.

This is the chapter I do not want to write and the chapter that must be written.

Every pilot, if they live long enough, will reach a point where they should no longer be flying combat. Not because they are a bad pilot, but because even their best adjusted flying is no longer adequate to the demands of the job. The decline has progressed past the point where judgment and patience can compensate. The engagements they are losing are engagements they should be winning, and no further adjustment will change that.

The hard truth is that most pilots do not know when they have reached this point.

The decline is gradual. Each loss, each mistake, is explicable in terms other than the pilot’s general decline. Bad luck. An unusually skilled opponent. A ship that was not at its best. The pilot’s own self-image, the image of themselves as a capable professional, is protected against the evidence of their decline.

And so the pilot keeps flying. Until, eventually, they do not come back.

I have watched this happen too many times in my career. Old pilots, good pilots, pilots who had earned the right to a safe retirement, killed on missions they should have retired from flying before. Each of them had reasons to continue flying. Each of them had overcome the warnings of wingmen and commanders and medical officers. And each of them, in the end, was wrong.

Know when to stop.

If you cannot know for yourself, and most pilots cannot, listen to the people around you. If your squadron commander suggests you move to an instructional role, consider the suggestion seriously. If your medical officer raises concerns about your fitness, do not dismiss them. If your wingman, in a private conversation, mentions that your flying has changed, take the conversation seriously.

These people are trying to save your life.

They may be wrong. They sometimes are. But they are a check on your self-assessment, and your self-assessment, as we have established, is the least reliable judge of your own decline.


## The Walk to the Ready Room

There comes a day, for every pilot who lives long enough to see it, when you make your final walk to the ready room.

Your last briefing. Your last flight. Your last landing.

I do not mean to be maudlin. I mean to be accurate. That day will come, for you, if you live. Either because you have chosen it, having judged that your time is up, or because it has been chosen for you, by a medical review or a commander’s decision.

If you are fortunate, you will see the day coming. You will know, in advance, that your next flight is your last, and you will be able to appreciate it accordingly. You will land a final time, taxi to the hangar, shut down your engines, and sit in the cockpit for a long moment before you climb out, knowing what the moment means.

If you are less fortunate, the day will arrive suddenly. A medical event. A squadron reassignment. A campaign that ends. You will not know, when you are flying, that this is the last flight. You will only realize, afterward, that it was.

Either way, the day comes.

The pilots who handle it well are the pilots who have understood, throughout their careers, that flying was a thing they did, not a thing they were. The flying ends. The pilot remains, as an instructor, as a commander, as a retiree, as someone who did something remarkable for a span of years and who will now do something else.

The pilots who handle it badly are the ones who confused the flying with their identity. For them, the end of flying is the end of themselves, and they are often lost in the years that follow.

Do not confuse the flying with yourself.

You are a person. The flying is a thing you do. When the flying ends, you will still be a person, and you will still have a life to live.

Fly as long as you are fit to fly. When you are not, stop.

And then go do whatever comes next, with the same commitment you brought to the cockpit.
