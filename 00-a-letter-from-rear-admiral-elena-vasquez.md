**FOREWORD**

# A Letter From Rear Admiral Elena Vasquez

The man whose story you are about to read refused for years to write this book. He wrote it in the end because he owed me, which he will not tell you but I will.

I flew under Commodore Thorne when I was a very young Lieutenant and he was already old, though he would dispute the word. I will not tell you the particulars of what passed between us in those years, partly because he tells some of it later in this book and tells it more honestly than I could, and partly because the particulars are ours and I find I want to keep them. I will tell you only that I owe him a great deal, and that I have spent the time since trying to be worth the trouble he once went to on my account. He knew me when I was a very serious new Lieutenant and not yet much else, and I have sometimes wished he had known the rest of me.

I did not stay in a cockpit. The dark took that from me, in the way it takes things, and the Fleet, unwilling to waste what it had spent training me, sent me to Caltech for a doctorate and set me to teaching advanced physics at the Academy, the physics underneath the flying I could no longer do. I have spent the years since teaching that physics to cadets, and then theorizing new tactics and ship design, and I now have the considerable honor of running the whole school. I tell you this not to self-aggrandize but so that you understand the position from which I say what I am about to say.

I can model the energy and the maneuver of a fighter to as many decimal places as you have patience for. I can prove to you, on a board, exactly why a turn costs what it costs. And I can tell you, as the person in the coalition perhaps best equipped to know it, that none of my equations will teach a frightened young Lieutenant when to break off and go home. There is a body of knowledge that does not reduce to mathematics, that lives only in the people who have flown long enough to have it and is lost entirely when they die. Bobby Thorne carries more of it than anyone I have known. That is why I went to him.

I asked him first to come and teach it himself, in person, to the cadets who need it. He declined. He is, as I write this, standing in a river in Montana with a fishing rod, having decided after forty years in the dark that he would prefer to spend his remaining time somewhere with weather, and I find I cannot argue with him. So I asked him a few times to do the next best thing, which was to set it down before it is lost, and to my genuine surprise he finally agreed, with as much grace as he agrees to anything, which is to say grudgingly and with a list of conditions I am still paying off.

You should know what kind of book this is, because it is not the kind most people expect from a man with his decorations. It is short. It is plain. It refuses to flatter you, and it will refuse to flatter you on the very first page, which I think is the most honest thing about it. He did not write it to be admired. He wrote it because he has watched too many young pilots die of things that did not have to kill them, and because the peace we have been living in is thinning, and he feels it the way old soldiers do. A long quiet is a poor teacher. Someone who remembers what the loud years cost ought to set it down now, while there is quiet enough to write and someone left who remembers.

This is not anything like your usual textbooks, read him the way you would listen to him on a flight line, which is to say closely, and without expecting comfort. He is right far more often than he is gentle. When he tells you that the most important person in your professional life is your wingman, believe him. He has reason to know, and so, as it happens, do I.

Fly well. Come home. He will tell you the rest.

— Rear Admiral Elena Vasquez

Superintendent, the Martial Academy
