**CHAPTER ELEVEN**

# The Briefing Room

THE briefing room is a place I have spent more of my life in than I have spent in my own home.

I have sat through briefings in more than thirty campaigns, in dozens of squadrons, under the direction of every conceivable kind of briefing officer, the meticulous ones, the hurried ones, the gifted ones, the incompetent ones. I have given briefings myself for more than fifteen years as a squadron commander and later as a staff officer.

And in all that time, I have observed one thing consistently: the pilots who get the most out of a briefing are the pilots who know how to sit in one.

This sounds, on its face, like a ridiculous claim. Sitting in a briefing is sitting in a briefing. You listen. You take notes. You ask questions when you have them. What is there to know?

There is, in fact, quite a lot to know. And most pilots, in my experience, do not know it.

This chapter is about how to take a briefing.


## What a Briefing Is

A briefing is not a lecture.

A briefing is a working meeting whose purpose is to transfer, in a short time, all of the tactical and operational information that the pilots need in order to execute their mission successfully and safely.

The briefing officer has this information. The pilots need it. The briefing is the mechanism by which the transfer occurs.

This means that a briefing is not successful simply because the briefing officer has delivered the information. A briefing is successful when the pilots have received it, understood it, and are prepared to act on it. And the pilots bear responsibility for that second half of the transaction.

If a pilot sits through a briefing and does not understand something, and does not ask about it, and then flies the mission and gets people killed because they were operating on a misunderstanding, the fault is partly theirs. The briefing officer may have been unclear, or too fast. But if the pilot had an opportunity to clarify, and chose not to, then responsibility for the failure is on them.

A pilot who treats the briefing as a passive experience, who sits there, who lets the information wash over them, who nods along without really engaging, is a pilot who is not getting what they should be getting from the briefing.

Do not be that pilot.


## Arriving at the Briefing

A pilot’s performance in a briefing begins before they walk in the door.

You arrive early. I mean this. You arrive five to ten minutes before the scheduled start. You find your seat. You get out your materials. You settle in. You are not the pilot who bursts into the briefing room three minutes late, still pulling on their flight jacket, disrupting the other pilots who are trying to prepare.

You arrive early because you need a few minutes of quiet before the briefing starts, to clear your mind and focus on what you are about to hear. You arrive early because the first few minutes of a briefing often contain the most important information, and the pilot who walks in late has already missed it. You arrive early because it is a mark of a professional.

You bring the materials you will need. A notepad, pencils (pens fail in cold cockpits), any personal navigation materials you use. If you have a specific question from a previous mission or from your own study, you bring a note of it, and you raise it at the appropriate time.

You have eaten. You are not dehydrated. You are not hung over. You are not distracted by some personal matter you have been unable to set aside. If you are any of these things, you owe it to your squadron to say so, and to be reassigned or scrubbed from the mission. A pilot who attends a briefing in poor condition and then flies in poor condition is a hazard to every other pilot on the mission.


## During the Briefing

During the briefing, you listen actively.

Active listening is different from passive listening, and the difference matters.

Passive listening is letting the information wash over you while you sit quietly. It is what most pilots do in most briefings. It produces the sensation of having attended the briefing without the substance of having received it. A pilot who has passively listened to a briefing can often repeat some of what was said, but they have not integrated it, and they will be surprised by things in the mission that were covered in the briefing but that they did not really absorb.

Active listening is engaging with the information as it is delivered. It means asking yourself, as each point is made, whether you understand it, whether you can visualize it, whether you can see yourself executing it. If you cannot do any of these things, you note the gap, and you ask a question when the opportunity arises.

Active listening means taking notes. Not transcribing the entire briefing, you do not have time, but noting the specific pieces of information that are mission-critical or that you might otherwise forget. Frequencies. Call signs. Waypoints. Threat axes. Abort criteria. These go in your notes. They go in your notes in a form you can reference quickly in the cockpit.

Active listening means watching the briefing officer. Their face, their gestures, their tone of voice will often communicate emphasis that the words themselves do not. A good briefing officer will be visibly more serious about some points than others. They will pause at critical moments. They will make eye contact during the most important warnings. Pay attention to these cues. They are telling you which pieces of information matter most.


## Asking Questions

The question-asking phase of a briefing is, in many squadrons, badly underused.

Young pilots do not ask questions because they are afraid of looking foolish. They think that asking a question reveals that they did not understand something, and they believe that not understanding something is shameful.

This is wrong. It is wrong for two reasons.

First, not understanding something is not shameful. Understanding everything in a complex briefing on the first pass is not a realistic expectation. The briefing officer has had hours to prepare. The pilots have had minutes. Of course there will be things that are unclear. Asking about them is not a sign of weakness; it is a sign of professional diligence.

Second, the pilot who does not ask questions is not the pilot who understood everything. They are the pilot who is going to execute on a misunderstanding, and whose misunderstanding will become apparent at some critical moment when it cannot be corrected. The question you do not ask in the briefing room is the question you will be asking in the cockpit, with the enemy in front of you and no one to answer it.

Ask questions. Ask them early. Ask them specifically.

A good question names the specific piece of information that is unclear. “Ma'am, on the recovery vector, is that a system-grid heading or relative to our own nose?” is a good question. “Could you go over the recovery again?” is a bad question, because it asks the briefing officer to repeat everything they have already said, rather than identifying the specific gap.

Senior pilots will note which new pilots are asking good questions and which are asking bad ones. The good-question ones will be marked as pilots worth developing. The bad-question ones will be marked as pilots who have not yet learned to listen. Again, this should not ever discourage you from asking a question. The goal is zero ambiguity. Just be sure to consistently improve your ability to ask it.


## After the Briefing

After the briefing, you do not immediately go to your ship.

You take five minutes. You sit somewhere quiet. You review your notes. You visualize the mission, from start to finish, in your mind’s eye. You see yourself performing each phase of it. You note anything that still does not make sense.

If anything still does not make sense, you track down the briefing officer and you ask them about it, privately, before you launch. A briefing officer who has just finished a briefing is usually available for a few minutes of one-on-one follow-up, and they will almost always prefer to clarify a misunderstanding on the ground than to have a pilot execute on it in flight.

If the briefing officer is not available, you find your flight lead, and you ask them. If they are not available, you ask your wingman. Do not launch a mission with an unresolved misunderstanding about the briefing. The mission is not worth more than the cost of a few minutes of clarification.


## The Briefings You Will Remember

Over the course of a career, you will sit through hundreds of briefings. Most of them will be forgettable. Some of them will be terrible. A few of them, the ones given by the truly great briefing officers, will be unforgettable.

The great briefings are the ones that leave you, when you walk out of the briefing room, with a clear picture of the mission in your mind. You can see the route. You can see the threats. You can see yourself responding to the contingencies that have been covered. You feel, walking to your ship, that you have been properly prepared for what is about to happen.

I remember perhaps a dozen such briefings from my entire career. I remember them because they are the exceptions. The vast majority of briefings I attended were adequate at best.

The implication of this is that you cannot count on the briefing to prepare you. You must be an active participant in your own preparation. You must supplement what the briefing provides with your own study, your own visualization, your own questions.

A passive pilot is a pilot who will be unprepared. An active pilot, a pilot who takes responsibility for their own understanding of the mission, is a pilot who will be prepared even when the briefing itself is inadequate.

Be the active pilot.

It is the only kind worth being.
