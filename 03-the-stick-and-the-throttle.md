_PART ONE_

**CHAPTER ONE**

# The Stick and the Throttle

EVERYTHING a fighter pilot does, they do with their hands.

I do not say this to be poetic. I say it because it is literally true, and because most of the rookies I have trained have not understood it in their bones until they have been told repeatedly and been made to demonstrate it repeatedly and been made to understand that all their other training, their navigation, their tactics, their gunnery, their communications, is transmitted from their brain to their ship through two pieces of hardware. The stick in their right hand. The throttle in their left.

If the connection between the pilot’s mind and those two pieces of hardware is poor, nothing else in the pilot’s training matters.

This is the first and most fundamental lesson of combat flying, and it is the one most consistently botched by flight schools, instructors, and pilots themselves. I have seen academy graduates who could recite the performance envelope of every fighter in the fleet but who held their stick like they were choking a goose. I have seen decorated veterans who had developed, over years of hard-won experience, habits of grip and throttle handling that were actively killing their performance, and who could not be persuaded to change them because they had gotten this far, hadn’t they?

They had gotten that far in spite of those habits, not because of them.

And they would have gotten further without them.

Let us begin, then, with the grip.


## The Grip

Your right hand is on the stick. This is the hand that flies the ship. Everything else, the throttle, the weapons, the comms, the navigation, is in service of what the stick is doing.

The stick is not a handle. It is not a lever. It is not a thing you pull on. The stick is, properly understood, a direct mechanical extension of your nervous system into the control surfaces of the ship. What your hand tells the stick, the stick tells the ship, and what the ship does, the stick feels and tells your hand back. It is a conversation, and it is continuous, and the quality of that conversation determines the quality of your flying.

A good grip on the stick is firm but not tight.

Say that back to yourself. Firm. But not tight.

A rookie’s instinct, the first time they strap into a real combat cockpit, is to squeeze the stick like they are trying to wring water from it. This is the worst possible thing they can do, for three reasons.

First, a tight grip fatigues the hand. Combat sorties run long, and a pilot who is squeezing their stick will have a hand full of cramps by the time they need it most. I have seen pilots lose engagements in the final minutes because their stick hand had gone numb.

Second, a tight grip deadens feel. The ship talks to you through the stick, through small vibrations, small resistances, small changes in the pressure it takes to hold a turn. If you are gripping the stick hard, you cannot hear what it is telling you. You are shouting in the conversation instead of listening.

Third, a tight grip makes your inputs coarse. Combat flying requires precision, and precision requires a delicate touch. A pilot who is white-knuckling their stick will over-correct, and over-correct again, and spend their entire fight oscillating around the control input they actually wanted to make.

The grip I teach is this. You hold the stick as you would hold a small bird. Firmly enough that it cannot fly away. Gently enough that you do not crush it.

I have used this analogy for thirty years and I have yet to find a better one. The bird does not want to be held. It will squirm. It will try to get free. Your job is to hold it steady, not by overpowering it, but by maintaining just enough pressure to keep it in your hand. That is the grip. That is the feel. That is the relationship between your hand and your stick for every moment of every sortie.

Your fingers wrap around the stick, not your palm. The stick should rest against the pads of your fingers and the fleshy part of your thumb, with the shaft of the stick lying in the channel formed between your thumb and forefinger. Your palm should barely be involved.

This is important and it is widely misunderstood.

A pilot who grips with their palm is flying with their arm. A pilot who grips with their fingers is flying with their hand and their wrist. The hand and the wrist are faster and more precise than the arm. The arm should be used only for gross movements, a hard bank, a violent reversal. For everything else, the hand and the wrist do the work.

Most rookies, if you watch their right arm during a training flight, are flying with their whole shoulder. Their elbow is locked. Their arm is stiff. They are moving the stick with their entire upper body, as if they were rowing a boat. This is how they have been taught to hold a heavy tool, and the stick is not a heavy tool. The stick is a conversation piece.

Relax the shoulder. Relax the elbow. Let the hand and the wrist do the work.


## The Throttle

Your left hand is on the throttle. This is the hand that controls your energy, and energy, as we will see in a later chapter, is the one currency that matters in a dogfight.

The throttle is simpler than the stick. It has one job, which is to tell the engines how much power to produce, and the pilot’s job is to tell the throttle what they want. But because the throttle is simpler, most pilots do not think about it enough, and this is where the throttle starts to kill them.

I have watched hundreds of rookies sit in the cockpit, and I can tell you that roughly nine out of ten of them, when things get interesting, forget that their left hand exists.

They slam the throttle to the firewall at the first sign of trouble, and they leave it there.

Understand what this means. It means that at the most critical moments of their flying careers, the moments when subtle energy management could save their lives, they have taken one of their two primary controls and reduced it to a binary switch. On. Off. Full burn or coast. A pilot who flies this way is flying with half the tools they were issued.

The grip on the throttle is like the grip on the stick, but even lighter. You do not need to squeeze the throttle. It is not going anywhere. A light hand resting on the throttle, with the fingers curled loosely around it, is all that is required. You should be able to move the throttle with the flick of a thumb or the bend of a wrist. You should never be moving it with your arm.

And you should be moving it constantly.

This is the hardest lesson in throttle handling, and the one that separates the journeymen from the aces. Your throttle hand is never still. In a fight, it is making micro-adjustments every second. Burning harder to close range, easing off to tighten a turn, pulsing to confuse a pursuer, holding at the optimal combat setting when you need raw performance. The throttle is not a volume knob. It is a musical instrument, and you play it the whole time you are in the air.

A pilot who masters their throttle has effectively added a third dimension to their combat envelope. Their turns are tighter because they can bleed energy on command. Their runs are faster because they can dump fuel into the engines at the right moment. Their escapes are cleaner because they have energy in reserve when everyone else has burned theirs.

A pilot who does not master their throttle is, in a very real sense, flying with one hand tied behind their back.


## Putting Them Together

The stick and the throttle must work together. This sounds obvious. It is not.

I have watched pilots, pilots with years of experience, pilots with combat awards, whose left and right hands appeared to belong to two different aviators. The stick hand would be pulling into a tight turn, and the throttle hand would be sitting at eighty percent, doing nothing. The throttle hand would be chopping for maneuver, and the stick hand would be holding a lazy course. The two hands were not cooperating. They were not even in the same conversation.

Good flying, the kind of flying that wins fights and brings you home, is a duet. The stick and the throttle are two instruments playing one tune. Every input on the stick should be accompanied by an appropriate adjustment on the throttle. Every throttle change should be reflected in how you handle the stick. They are not independent systems. They are two halves of a single action, which is the action of flying the ship.

When I watch a young pilot fly, the first thing I look at is not their turns or their shooting or their positioning.

I look at their hands.

If their hands are working together, the right hand firm but not tight, the left hand light and active, both hands in the same conversation, then everything else can be taught. If their hands are fighting each other, or if one hand is idle while the other does all the work, then nothing else matters. That pilot has not yet learned to fly, and they will not be learning anything useful until they do.

Fix your hands first.

Everything else follows.
