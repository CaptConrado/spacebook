**CHAPTER SEVEN**

# Shooting Straight

I HAVE been waiting, through the first six chapters of this book, to talk about gunnery.

I have been waiting because gunnery is the subject that every young pilot wants to talk about first, and it is the subject that every experienced instructor wants to talk about last. There is a reason for this disparity, and I want you to understand it before we go any further.

The reason is that gunnery, in and of itself, is not difficult.

The mechanics of aiming a gun and firing it at a moving target can be taught to a competent student in a few weeks. The mathematics of lead and range and angle can be learned from a training manual. The feel for when to fire and when to hold comes with a hundred or so sorties of guncam review.

What is difficult is not the gunnery. What is difficult is everything that comes before the gunnery, the flying, the positioning, the energy management, the tactical awareness that puts you into a position where gunnery is even possible. A pilot with perfect gunnery and terrible flying will never get a shot off. A pilot with adequate gunnery and excellent flying will make a kill on most sorties.

This is why the instruction in gunnery comes after the instruction in everything else.

Nevertheless. Gunnery matters, and it is a discipline that can be studied and improved, and the pilot who puts in the work on their gunnery will convert more of their shooting opportunities into kills than the pilot who does not.

So. Let us talk about how to shoot straight.


## The Three Components of a Shot

Every shot you will ever take in combat has three components. They are, in order of importance:

One. Position. You must be in a geometric position where a hit is possible. No amount of gunnery skill can hit a target that is not within your weapons envelope. Position is the first requirement. Without position, nothing else matters.

Two. Tracking. You must be able to keep your guns on the target long enough to fire a useful burst. Tracking is the art of matching your ship’s motion to the target’s motion, so that your sight picture is stable, or as stable as it can be, for the moments before and during the shot.

Three. Trigger discipline. You must fire at the right moment, for the right duration, with the right burst length. You must neither fire too early (wasting ammunition on a poor shot) nor too late (missing the opportunity entirely), nor fire for too long (overheating your guns or running your ammunition dry before the fight is over).

All three components must be present for a successful shot. If any one is missing, the shot fails.

Most rookies focus on trigger discipline, because it is the most obvious component. They think of gunnery as a matter of pulling the trigger at the right moment. They are wrong. Trigger discipline is the easiest component to learn, and it is the least important.

Position and tracking are the hard parts. And they are also, not coincidentally, the parts that come from good flying rather than from shooting practice.


## Lead

Let me talk for a moment about lead, because this is the concept that rookies most frequently misunderstand.

Lead is the amount you aim ahead of the target to account for the time it takes your projectiles to reach it. If the target is moving, and your projectile takes time to travel, you cannot aim directly at the target. You must aim at where the target will be by the time the projectile arrives.

This is straightforward in principle. In practice, it is the source of ninety percent of the missed shots in combat.

The problem is that lead depends on three variables, and all three are changing constantly.

The first variable is range. The farther away the target is, the longer your projectile takes to reach it, and the more lead you must apply.

The second variable is the target’s crossing speed, the component of its velocity that is perpendicular to your line of fire. A target moving directly toward you or away from you requires no lead at all. A target moving across your line of sight requires substantial lead.

The third variable is your own velocity. This is where rookies often go wrong. They think of lead as a property of the target, but it is actually a property of the relative motion between your ship and the target. If you are both moving at high speed in the same direction, there may be very little effective crossing speed, and very little lead required. If you are moving in opposite directions, the effective crossing speed is enormous, and the required lead is substantial.

Your gunsight will compute lead for you. Every modern fighter has a computer that takes in the target’s position and velocity and shows you where to aim. You would think this would solve the problem.

It does not.

The computer assumes that the target’s current velocity is its future velocity. But pilots maneuver. A target that was moving left when the computer computed its lead may be moving right by the time your projectiles arrive. The computer’s solution is only as good as its most recent data, and its most recent data is always slightly stale.

This means that the pilot still has to think.

You use the gunsight as a starting point. You apply your own judgment, based on your read of the target’s probable maneuvers, to refine your aim. And you fire only when your judgment and the gunsight agree.

A gunsight is an aid. It is not a substitute for thinking.


## Burst Length

Fire in short bursts. This is the oldest advice in combat gunnery and the one most frequently ignored.

A short burst is one to two seconds of fire. It sends enough projectiles to produce a good kill probability if the aim is correct, and it conserves ammunition if the aim is wrong.

A long burst, more than three seconds, does almost nothing useful. After the first second or two of fire, your guns begin to drift from their initial aim point, due to vibration, ship motion, and the inherent dispersion of the weapons. Projectiles fired after that initial window are statistically unlikely to hit, even if the first projectiles did. You are wasting ammunition.

Moreover, long bursts heat up your guns. Most fighter weapons systems have thermal limits, and a pilot who fires their guns continuously for several seconds may find themselves, in the next pass, with a weapons system that is offline for cooling.

Short bursts. Two seconds. No more. If the first burst missed, you do not continue firing, you cease fire, reassess, reposition, and set up another shot.

Rookies hold the trigger down. They empty their magazines at a target that is not in their sight picture. They come home with no ammunition and no kills and they wonder what went wrong.

What went wrong is that they were never taught the discipline of the short burst, and they have not yet learned it through experience, and many of them will not live long enough to learn it.

Do not be one of those rookies.


## The Deflection Shot

The deflection shot is a shot taken at a target crossing your line of fire at an angle. It is the most common type of shot in combat, far more common than the straight-on shot or the straight-tail shot, and it is also the most difficult.

The deflection shot requires good lead, good tracking, and good timing, all three simultaneously, and it is unforgiving of errors in any of them.

It also requires the pilot to maintain their aim through a turn, which is mechanically harder than maintaining aim in level flight. In a deflection shot, you are typically turning to track the target, and your own ship’s motion is contributing to the geometry. A small error in your turn rate produces a large error in your sight picture.

The deflection shot is learned through practice. I do not have a clever shortcut to offer you. You must take the deflection shot, and miss, and take it again, and miss, and gradually develop a feel for how your ship behaves in the turn and how the sight picture evolves as the geometry changes.

What I will tell you is this. The deflection shot is where the aces are made. A pilot who masters the deflection shot has access to kills that other pilots cannot make. They do not have to get directly behind their targets. They do not have to set up the perfect tail chase. They can shoot from angles that, to a poorer gunner, would look like impossible shots.

Learn the deflection shot. It is worth ten of the straight shots.


## Three Good Bursts

I want to close this chapter with the rule I was given by Blacksmith Moray, who in turn got it from his instructor, who got it from his, going back before any of us were flying.

A good pilot fires three bursts per engagement.

The first burst establishes the terms. It tells the enemy you will shoot, as I discussed in the merge chapter.

The second burst is the considered shot, taken when the geometry is good, with careful aim.

The third burst is the finishing shot, taken when the enemy is either damaged or out of position, to seal the kill.

Three bursts. Two seconds each. Six seconds of trigger time in an engagement that may last two minutes.

If you find yourself firing more than three bursts in a single engagement, you are probably firing shots you should not be taking. You are giving in to the temptation to shoot at targets you have not properly set up. You are wasting ammunition on marginal geometry. You are, in short, shooting instead of flying, and the shooting is distracting you from the flying, and the flying is what will actually win the fight.

Discipline your trigger finger. Shoot only the shots that are worth taking. Trust your flying to put you in position for shots that are worth taking.

Three bursts. And then you are home.
